# Documented Kalshi edges die at execution

The public record on Kalshi and prediction-market edges converges, independently and repeatedly, on the same conclusion this project already reached the hard way: real, statistically detectable mispricings exist — a favourite-longshot bias, in-play underreaction, parlay overpricing — but every study that actually computes an executable, cost-inclusive return finds the edge destroyed by the bid-ask spread and fees, not merely dented by them. The open-source ecosystem around Kalshi is thin, largely unaudited, and offers little beyond a few genuinely useful public datasets and reference API clients; no backtesting harness in public view rivals what this repo already has. The academic literature (three 2026 arXiv preprints, two SSRN working papers, one Federal Reserve working paper) confirms the shape of the bias but is split on how to explain who profits from it, and the apparent conflict between a widely-cited maker-return finding and this project's own maker simulation resolves once the comparison is made price-band-for-price-band rather than headline-to-headline. Kalshi's own liquidity-incentive program, not raw mispricing-hunting, is the most concretely evidenced route to exchange-subsidized income for a small participant. The most promising unexplored leads — parlay overpricing and a decaying Fed-funds-futures basis trade — are both outside the reader's currently accessible categories or already degrading. And a realtime terminal is straightforward to build technically but requires crossing a threshold this project has so far avoided: Kalshi's WebSocket API demands an authenticated, credentialed connection even for public market data, so a "live terminal" is a different trust posture than the credential-free pipelines built to date. Several of the most-cited papers below (Bürgi/Deng/Whelan, Bartlett & O'Hara, the weather-hedging paper) could only be verified through search-engine-surfaced abstracts because their PDFs returned 403 or 429 on direct fetch — a limitation that matters more than usual here, since the precise numbers from those papers are load-bearing for the report's conclusions.

## The open-source ecosystem is thin, unaudited, and mostly not worth adopting

Kalshi's own code presence is minimal: `Kalshi/kalshi-starter-code-python` is the only repo under the official GitHub org, and it has sat unpushed for over a year (last push 2025-03-07) ([Kalshi/kalshi-starter-code-python](https://github.com/Kalshi/kalshi-starter-code-python)). The more actively maintained clients are unofficial — `arshka/pykalshi` (125 stars, MIT, pushed 2026-07-29, WebSocket streaming and automatic retries) and `TexasCoding/kalshi-python-sdk` (an async FIX-engine implementation, but only 6 stars, meaning "active" here means one person's recent commits rather than community adoption) ([arshka/pykalshi](https://github.com/arshka/pykalshi); [TexasCoding/kalshi-python-sdk](https://github.com/TexasCoding/kalshi-python-sdk)). The much larger population of "Kalshi trading bot" repos — `LoQiseaking69/kalshi-trading-bot`, `yllvar/Kalshi-Quant-TeleBot`, `brandononchain/kalshibot`, and similar — carry self-descriptions like "enterprise-grade" and "institutional-quality" but disclose no real account statements, no audited P&L, and in several cases no license at all; none of this is proof of a working edge, and star counts under 50 with single-digit contributor rosters should be read as "solo weekend project," not "vetted infrastructure." On the Polymarket side, tooling is more active but structurally unstable: the flagship official client `Polymarket/py-clob-client` (1,231 stars) is archived, with Polymarket's own README now redirecting integrators to `Polymarket/py-sdk`, and the widely-starred `Polymarket/agents` repo (3,793 stars) is also archived and two years stale — a reminder that popularity and current maintenance are different things on this beat ([Polymarket/py-clob-client](https://github.com/Polymarket/py-clob-client); [Polymarket/agents](https://github.com/Polymarket/agents)). The one clearly high-value find is `Jon-Becker/prediction-market-analysis`, an MIT-licensed, actively maintained (pushed 2026-08-10) framework advertised as "the largest publicly available dataset of Polymarket and Kalshi market and trade data," with 3,844 stars — GitHub metadata confirmed directly against the API rather than through search summaries ([Jon-Becker/prediction-market-analysis](https://github.com/jon-becker/prediction-market-analysis)). A second, more academically grounded data source is `jdkatz21/Prediction_Markets_Public`, the replication package behind a Fed/Johns Hopkins-adjacent "Rise in Macro Markets" project (Diercks, Katz, Wright); its 45 stars is modest, but its methodology for converting raw Kalshi trades into calibrated forecasts is a credible reference to sanity-check this project's own pipeline against ([jdkatz21/Prediction_Markets_Public](https://github.com/jdkatz21/Prediction_Markets_Public)). No general-purpose, well-adopted Kalshi backtesting engine with fee and fill modelling exists in public view — the closest analogues are ad hoc modules bundled inside individual (Polymarket-focused) bot repos — which means this project's paper broker and event-clustered statistics are not duplicating anything better that already exists; they are, on the present evidence, ahead of the public field on rigor even if not on line count.

## Three 2026 papers land on the same verdict, and the maker paradox is not really a paradox

The strongest available evidence is unusually convergent for such a young research area. A Polymarket study of 588 million trades finds contracts below 10 cents lose 19.3 cents per dollar while contracts at or above 90 cents earn a small positive return, with the pattern robust in crypto and politics but "surprisingly absent in Sports" ([arXiv:2609.12878](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12878)). A separate 292-million-trade Kalshi-plus-Polymarket calibration study finds a domain-specific pattern that cuts against a single universal story: **Politics shows persistent long-horizon underconfidence** (intercept +0.151), **Sports is essentially well-calibrated** (+0.010, indistinguishable from zero), and **Weather shows short-horizon overconfidence** — the opposite sign from the classic bias — with slopes of 0.69–0.97 inside 48 hours ([arXiv:2602.19520](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.19520v1)). That paper's implied 5-point price error at a one-month horizon (a 70-cent contract corresponding to a true 75% probability) sits well inside typical spreads at that horizon — the same "bias exists but is smaller than the spread" shape this project independently found across 9,252 weather brackets. The most Kalshi-specific and most cited working paper, Bürgi, Deng & Whelan's "Makers and Takers" (SSRN 5502658, using Kalshi's inception-to-April-2025 data and its own maker/taker trade-side field), reports an overall average pre-fee return of roughly **-20%**, with **Makers averaging -9.64%** post-fee versus **Takers averaging -31.46%**, and Makers on contracts priced **≥50 cents averaging +2.6% with a 33% standard deviation** ([SSRN 5502658 abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5502658)) — note that this paper's full text could not be independently verified in this research pass (both direct and Delivery.cfm PDF fetches returned 403), so the exact figures rest on search-surfaced abstract and secondary-summary text, not a primary read.

That +2.6% maker figure looks, at first glance, to contradict this project's own maker simulation, which found passive selling of cheap weather brackets loses money (-1.9% ROI) under perfect adverse selection — 100% of eventual winners filled against only 26.8% of losers. The resolution, worked out directly against the paper's abstract-level claims, is that these are not comparable numbers. Selling a 3-cent YES bracket is mechanically the same position as buying a 97-cent NO contract, which falls in the paper's ≥50-cent bucket — so the correct comparison is to the +2.6% figure with its 33% standard deviation, an enormous dispersion around a thin mean that is exactly the tail-risk shape already measured here (a handful of ~32-cent wins against rarer ~946-cent losses). The paper's own category breakdown independently supports this project's finding that weather is comparatively well-priced: its favourite-longshot coefficient for climate/weather (ψ≈0.031) is one of the smallest of any category, similar to financials (0.032) and well below crypto (0.058), leaving less room for either side to profit net of the spread. And the paper's sample predates April 2025, when Kalshi began charging makers fees for the first time — meaning any historical +2.6% maker edge is a pre-fee upper bound, not a number achievable under the current fee schedule. Put together, the two findings describe the same underlying phenomenon at different price bands: this project tested the ask side of cheap longshot brackets and found it loses; the literature's only positive cell is the bid side of expensive contracts, which this project has not yet fill-simulated. A raw, fill-free pass over the same data already shows buying YES at the bid in the 40–60 cent band earning +2.68 cents per contract before fill modelling — the direct, not-yet-tested analogue of the paper's one positive result, and the single highest-value open experiment this research surfaced.

A second rigorous paper, Bartlett & O'Hara's "Adverse Selection in Prediction Markets: Evidence from Kalshi" (41.6 million trades, SSRN 6615739), adds an academic frame for exactly the market type this project trades: single-name, idiosyncratic markets like weather brackets show roughly **twice the informed-trader price impact** of broad-based markets, but market makers are compensated by a larger behavioral overpricing of YES by retail — a cross-subsidy that makes maker economics work in aggregate but says nothing about whether a small, undiversified maker (rather than a large one netting inventory across many markets) can reliably capture it ([SSRN 6615739 via Stanford Law press summary](https://law.stanford.edu/press/adverse-selection-in-prediction-markets-evidence-from-kalshi/); full text also 403, abstract-level only). A third paper closes the loop with the most directly comparable cost-inclusive test found anywhere in this research: an NBA in-game study (409,512 contract-minute observations, April 2025–May 2026) finds Kalshi prices genuinely underreact to public information (a 0.64-for-1 contemporaneous response, worse in clutch time), producing real predictable drift over the next 5–15 minutes — but "executable-style returns that buy at the ask and sell at the bid are negative," meaning the spread more than absorbs a statistically real, well-documented inefficiency ([arXiv:2606.07811](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.07811)). This is the closest published analogue to this project's own weather-bracket and NBA results, and it independently generalizes the pattern to a second market category using a different methodology.

## Kalshi pays for liquidity, not for finding mispricings

The most concretely evidenced income mechanism for a small participant is not strategy discovery but Kalshi's own **Liquidity Incentive Program**, which pays $1–$1,000 per market per day from a reward pool (not a fee rebate) to any eligible U.S. account posting resting two-sided orders of 100–20,000 contracts that improve book depth, running through January 2027 and open to ordinary account holders rather than only designated market makers ([Kalshi Help Center: Liquidity Incentive Program](https://help.kalshi.com/en/articles/13823851-liquidity-incentive-program)). Kalshi's separate, discretionary **Market Maker Program** requires a financial-resources review and 98% quoting uptime across covered products, and applicants "may be waitlisted indefinitely," making it a much less realistic entry point for a solo developer ([Kalshi Help Center: Market Maker Program](https://help.kalshi.com/en/articles/13823819-how-to-become-a-market-maker-on-kalshi)). Because rebate rates in both programs are communicated privately and adjustable on seven days' notice, any strategy built around "the subsidy exceeds my adverse-selection cost" needs continuous re-validation rather than a one-time check.

Cross-venue arbitrage between Kalshi and Polymarket is real but visibly compressing: marketing-oriented scanners claim average rates near 9.6% falling toward 3–8% "as more bots enter," and a more careful independent walkthrough found that of five apparent overnight arbitrage candidates, only three survived scrutiny once contract wording and mutual-exclusivity were checked — one of the "best-looking" signals was not an arbitrage at all ([algocapital.substack.com](https://algocapital.substack.com/p/what-happens-when-polymarket-and)). Institutional capital has arrived in force: Jump Trading took equity stakes in both venues and doubled its prediction-markets team in 2026, Susquehanna has disclosed a Kalshi market-making role since 2024, and Jane Street, Wintermute, DRW and IMC are reported to be entering as well ([Finance Magnates on Jump Trading](https://www.financemagnates.com/forex/prop-firm-jump-trading-to-take-stakes-in-kalshi-and-polymarket-report/)) — a strong signal that easily-scanned arbitrage in liquid markets is being competed down quickly, leaving thin or newly-listed markets as the more plausible remaining territory. Latency-based edges are not realistic for a retail participant on a home connection: physical fiber latency runs roughly 1 millisecond per 124 miles, so even a nearby colocation implies a floor around 10 milliseconds round-trip, and no credible source claims retail infrastructure can win a speed race against firms now running dedicated prediction-market desks. On tax treatment, Section 1256's favorable 60/40 capital-gains rate is an unresolved, aggressive position for Kalshi contracts as of 2026 — the IRS has issued no direct guidance, and Kalshi's own litigation posture (arguing its contracts are *not* swaps, to preserve its right to list sports and political markets) sits in direct tension with the swaps-exclusion clause that would need to be avoided for 1256 to apply, meaning a conservative ordinary-gains treatment is the lower-risk default ([Coselite: Section 1256 and Event Contracts](https://coselite.com/blog/section-1256-event-contracts)). Finally, the research turned up no verifiable, non-marketing account of any solo trader's real, sustained Kalshi profitability — every "$1,000 to $200,000" style claim traces back to SEO/content-marketing blogs with no audited records, which is itself informative: an active content ecosystem promoting Kalshi profitability, combined with a complete absence of auditable success stories, is consistent with the academic finding that the one documented positive edge (+2.6% Maker return on ≥50-cent contracts) is thin, high-variance, and capacity-constrained by median contract volumes near $9,000.

## The two least-picked-over leads sit just outside easy reach

Two candidate edges stand out from the rest for being large, documented, and not yet backtested net of costs by anyone — but both come with a catch relevant to this reader specifically. The first is **parlay overpricing**: cross-game NBA/MLB/NHL parlays on Kalshi are systematically overpriced relative to the product of their leg prices, growing roughly 3% per additional leg and reaching **22–31% overpricing at 10–11 legs**, stated before exchange fees ([arXiv:2607.14430](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14430v1)). No paper backtests actually selling into this net of fees and correlation risk, which makes it the single most promising *unexplored* lead in the literature — but it lives entirely inside Kalshi's sports category, which is geofenced off in Washington State, closing this door for now absent a change in the reader's jurisdiction or Kalshi's state-level offerings. The second is a **Fed-funds-futures basis trade**: a Federal Reserve working paper found Kalshi's Fed-rate forecasts beat CME Fed funds futures in accuracy immediately before FOMC meetings, but a public GitHub backtest of the same basis found the edge was real in the 2022–2025 "pinned" effective-fed-funds-rate regime and is "not distinguishable from noise" in the regime that began in September 2025, when EFFR started drifting intra-month ([GitHub: fed-basis-backtest](https://github.com/YichengYang-Ethan/fed-basis-backtest)) — a documented edge that appears to already be closing, in a category (economics) the reader can actually trade. Beyond trading, the clearest non-trading path that reuses this project's existing infrastructure almost as-is is a public calibration/accuracy writeup in the mold of Calibration City or Kalshi's own research vertical ([Kalshi Research: Calibration](https://kalshi.com/research/publications/calibration)) — this project's git history of honestly reported negative results (the weather model losing to market, favourite-longshot bias existing but sitting inside the spread) is already close to publishable research, and portfolio-construction guidance for quant hiring explicitly values reproducible, methodologically rigorous negative results over headline returns, which is precisely this project's current shape rather than a gap to fill ([GitHub: berryO307/quant-portfolio](https://github.com/berryO307/quant-portfolio)). Adjacent markets entirely — sports-betting arbitrage tooling (saturated and geofenced), retail weather derivatives (structurally closed to retail via CME/OTC institutional access), crypto basis trading (technically similar skill set, but yields have compressed roughly 3–6x from 30–50% in 2020–2021 to 5–15% annualized now) — were all considered and none offer a clearly better risk-adjusted opportunity than continuing to mine Kalshi's thinner categories.

## A live terminal is buildable, but it changes this project's trust model

Kalshi's WebSocket API is well-documented and directly matches most of what a research terminal needs: public channels for `ticker`, `trade`, `orderbook_delta`/snapshot, and `market_lifecycle_v2` cover spread, last price, open interest, book depth, and settlement-state transitions without any additional data acquisition work ([Kalshi Docs: Quick Start WebSockets](https://docs.kalshi.com/getting_started/quick_start_websockets)). The one point verified directly against Kalshi's own documentation index, and worth treating as a hard constraint rather than a detail: **every WebSocket connection, including for public market data, requires API-key authentication (RSA-PSS signed headers) during the handshake** — there is no anonymous firehose to tap, which means a live terminal is the first piece of this project that cannot be built credential-free the way the historical-candle and public-data pipelines have been. REST rate limits are generous enough (Basic tier ≈20 requests/second) to serve as a polling fallback if a socket drops, but no explicit per-connection websocket message-rate limit is documented, only an account-level 200-connection cap and an undocumented "subscription buffer overflow" error — worth testing empirically before subscribing to more than a handful of markets on one connection.

On prior art: OpenBB Terminal, the closest thing to a general "Bloomberg for retail" open-source project, was sunset in 2025–2026 specifically because of dependency bloat (500+ packages) and an inability to monetize a terminal built on data it didn't own, serving only 7,000–10,000 monthly active users despite years of development ([OpenBB: Sunsetting OpenBB Terminal](https://openbb.co/blog/sunsetting-openbb-terminal-why-how-and-what-now/)) — a direct cautionary tale about scope discipline for a solo builder, and it has no prediction-market module regardless. Purpose-built Kalshi/Polymarket dashboards exist on GitHub (`braedonsaunders/homerun`, 179 stars, FastAPI/React/Docker, claiming Cox proportional-hazards fill models and L2 order-book replay), but the identical marketing copy ("25+ built-in strategies, copy trading, AI scoring... One-click setup") appears verbatim under at least two other unrelated GitHub account names, a strong signal of template-farming or SEO duplication that should make anyone skeptical of the underlying feature claims even where the code looks structurally sophisticated ([braedonsaunders/homerun](https://github.com/braedonsaunders/homerun)). For the interface layer itself, Textual — a Python TUI framework built on Rich — is the lowest-friction choice given this project's existing Python codebase: it supports reactive, async/websocket-driven widgets natively, runs in a real terminal or over SSH, and can be served to a browser later via `textual-serve` without a rewrite ([Real Python: Python Textual](https://realpython.com/python-textual/)). For storage, SQLite or DuckDB at laptop scale is the right call — no server process, no ops burden — with TimescaleDB's partitioning/sharding machinery aimed at multi-node production deployments this project doesn't need ([pythondataengineering.net: DuckDB vs TimescaleDB](https://pythondataengineering.net/compare/duckdb-vs-timescaledb)); no primary source gave real observed message-rate or byte-volume figures for Kalshi's feed at any market count, so actual storage sizing remains a one-day instrumentation task rather than a researched fact. The terminal's most useful non-decorative feature, beyond live orderbook and spread display, is a fee-aware bracket-arbitrage check run on best-bid and best-ask sums separately (buy-the-basket should sum to ≥100 cents, sell-the-basket to ≤100 cents, absent an opportunity) gated on Kalshi's mutual-exclusivity flag — this project's own commit history already shows the fee-modelling discipline (charging Kalshi's fee once per order, not per contract) needed to make that check honest rather than a false-positive generator.

## Conclusion

The through-line across four independent research streams — open-source code, academic preprints, practitioner postmortems, and adjacent-market surveys — is that Kalshi's public mispricings are real but consistently smaller than the cost of accessing them for anyone without institutional quoting infrastructure, and that this project's own dead ends (weather loses, NBA loses, bias inside the spread, passive selling adversely selected) are not idiosyncratic failures but the same result independently reached by peer-reviewed-adjacent researchers using entirely different data and methods. The one genuinely new thing this research adds beyond confirmation is a specific, falsifiable next experiment — fill-simulating the bid side of 40–60 cent contracts — that follows directly from reconciling a headline academic number against this project's own price-band-specific result rather than treating them as in conflict. The second genuinely new thing is a reframing of value: given how saturated and unverifiable the "how to make money on Kalshi" content ecosystem is, and how consistently rigorous negative results are treated as credible signal rather than failure in quant-hiring contexts, this project's most differentiated output may not be a strategy that finally works, but the rare, honestly-documented demonstration that most plausible ones don't — a positioning a realtime terminal would make more vivid, not replace.
