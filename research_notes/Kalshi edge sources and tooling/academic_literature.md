# Academic/Published Evidence on Prediction-Market Inefficiencies (2023-2026)

Research conducted 2026-09-18. Scope: modern venues (Kalshi, Polymarket, event-contract exchanges), 2023-2026 preprints/working papers, with older horse-racing literature excluded per instructions. Note on source quality: most citable, quantitative material below comes from arXiv preprints and SSRN working papers that are **not yet peer-reviewed**; this is flagged inline. A few lower-rigor blog/aggregator sources turned up in searches and are explicitly excluded from Cited Findings or marked as unverified.

## Favourite-longshot bias in prediction markets: magnitude, and does it survive spread/costs?

### Takeaway
Multiple independent 2026 studies (Kalshi- and Polymarket-specific, N in the hundreds of millions of trades) confirm a favourite-longshot bias that is large in raw win-rate terms (longshots losing 6-19 cents per dollar depending on methodology) but none of the papers found demonstrate that a *taker* strategy net of realistic spread/fees is profitable — several explicitly show the opposite once fees or bid-ask spread are modeled, consistent with the reader's own 9,252-market finding that the bias is smaller than the spread.

### Cited Findings
- Polymarket, 588 million trades by 2.48 million accounts: "purchases below 10 cents lose 19.3 cents per dollar, while purchases at or above 90 cents earn 0.83 cents" per dollar (equal-weighted by contract) — but when grouped by parent event, longshots "lose 6.3 cents per dollar" while related-contract grouping shows "gain 4.1 cents" for some subsets; the two-sided pattern is robust in Crypto and Politics but "surprisingly absent in Sports." — [The Favorite-Longshot Bias in Prediction Markets: Evidence from Polymarket, arXiv:2609.12878](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12878), accessed 2026-09-18. (Preprint, unverified peer review.)
- Same paper: top-decile longshot-preferring traders (26.6% of low-price purchases) and top-decile favorite-preferring traders (15.1% of high-price purchases) both "earn similar returns" to or less than other traders, i.e. exploiting the aggregate bias as a naive strategy does not obviously generate outsized trader-level profit — [arXiv:2609.12878](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12878), accessed 2026-09-18.
- Kalshi + Polymarket combined, 292 million trades across 327,000 binary contracts through Dec 31, 2025 (64.7M trades / 210,608 contracts on Kalshi; 227.6M trades / 116,000 contracts on Polymarket): a logistic-recalibration slope (b=1 is perfect calibration, b>1 = underconfidence/prices compressed toward 50%) rises "from 0.99 (within one hour of resolution) to 1.32 (beyond one month)" — i.e. favourite-longshot-style underconfidence is a long-horizon phenomenon that shrinks to near-zero close to expiry. At one month+ horizon, "a contract trading at 70 cents one month out corresponds to a true probability closer to 75%" — a 5-point gap, well inside typical spreads for that horizon. — [Decomposing Crowd Wisdom: Domain-Specific Calibration Dynamics in Prediction Markets, arXiv:2602.19520](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.19520v1), accessed 2026-09-18. (Preprint.)
- Kalshi-specific (Bürgi, Deng, Whelan, "Makers and Takers: The Economics of the Kalshi Prediction Market," >300,000 contracts, SSRN 5502658 / UCD WP2025_19): abstract-level finding is that "Kalshi's contract prices are informative and improve in accuracy as markets approach closing, but display a clear favorite-longshot bias: low-price contracts win far less often than required to break even, while high-price contracts win more often and yield small positive returns." The paper's core contribution is a maker/taker matching model in which makers (who post the bid-ask spread) are the ones who can be "slightly over-optimistic" and still earn positive expected return, while takers face the bias net of the spread. — [SSRN 5502658 abstract via search](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/SSRN_ID5502658_code4203760.pdf?abstractid=5502658&mirid=1), accessed 2026-09-18 (full text returned 403/binary-garbled on fetch; findings taken from search-engine-surfaced abstract text, not independently verified against full PDF — mark as **partially unverified**, only the abstract-level claim is attributed here).
- Sports specifically (NBA/MLB/NHL moneylines on Kalshi, ~23 million trades, March-May 2026): near-expiry the bias/miscalibration becomes extreme rather than disappearing — in the "final 10 minutes," a Platt-scale slope parameter reaches "1.62 (NBA) to 4.56 (NHL)," and "contracts trading at p=0.4 have empirical win rates near zero." Crucially, the paper explicitly "excludes exchange fees" from its reported prices, i.e. the documented mispricing is pre-cost. — [Prices, Probabilities, and Parlays: Systematic Bias in Sports Prediction Markets, arXiv:2607.14430](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14430v1), accessed 2026-09-18. (Preprint.)

### Inferences
- The direction and rough order of magnitude of the bias (longshots overpriced, favourites underpriced) replicates across three independent 2026 studies and two venues, which is reassuring for the reader's own empirical finding — but no paper reviewed reports a taker-side backtest that survives spread/fees and turns a profit. Where papers do model costs (sports moneyline paper) they find the bias is stated *before* fees, and one paper on NBA in-game drift (below, under "post-news drift") explicitly finds executable buy-at-ask/sell-at-bid returns go negative.
- The longest-horizon bias (1+ month out) is the largest in relative calibration-slope terms but translates to only ~5 percentage points of price error at 70c — likely inside the spread for a market that far from resolution, similar to the reader's own spread-vs-bias comparison.
- The sports/near-expiry finding (extreme miscalibration in the last 10 minutes) is the most interesting divergence from "bias shrinks toward expiry" — it suggests a distinct near-expiry phenomenon (see below) that is different in kind from the classic long-horizon favourite-longshot bias.

### Gaps
- No paper found reports a full, cost-inclusive backtest of a "buy favourites systematically" or "avoid longshots systematically" strategy on Kalshi with realistic taker fees, sized to a solo trader's typical order size (which would face slippage beyond top-of-book).
- The Bürgi/Deng/Whelan full-text (spread size, maker vs taker realized returns in cents) could not be verified — both the karlwhelan.com and ucd.ie PDF mirrors failed to parse as text and the SSRN Delivery.cfm link returned 403. Only the abstract-level claim above is confirmed.

## Calibration studies of Kalshi/Polymarket: quality by category, time-to-expiry, and systematic biases

### Takeaway
Aggregate Kalshi/Polymarket calibration is good and improves monotonically toward expiry (Kalshi's own research reports Brier scores falling from ~0.09 at three months to <0.02 at close), but calibration quality is domain-dependent: Politics shows persistent long-horizon underconfidence, Weather shows short-horizon *overconfidence* (the opposite sign), and Sports is well-calibrated short-term but drifts to severe long-horizon underconfidence and to a distinct near-expiry breakdown.

### Cited Findings
- Kalshi's own research states: "Across 2,243,741 resolved Kalshi markets from 2021 to mid-2026, prices are well calibrated and increasingly so as resolution approaches: Brier scores fall from about 0.09 at three months to below 0.02 at close." — [Calibration in Prediction Markets: Theory and Evidence, Kalshi Research](https://kalshi.com/research/publications/calibration), surfaced via web search 2026-09-18; direct PDF fetch was rate-limited (HTTP 429) so this is drawn from the search-engine summary of Kalshi's own publication, not independently re-verified from the primary PDF — **flag as company-published, not independently peer-reviewed, and not directly re-fetched.**
- Domain-specific calibration (292M trades, Kalshi+Polymarket, through Dec 2025): a four-component decomposition (domain, horizon, trade-size, platform) explains "87.3% of calibration variance across 216 cells," with a Bayesian hierarchical model achieving "96.3% posterior predictive coverage." Category-level intercepts: Politics +0.151 [+0.122, +0.179] (persistent underconfidence — a 70c political contract one week out corresponds to ~83% true probability); Sports +0.010 [-0.020, +0.039] (indistinguishable from zero, i.e. well-calibrated, short/medium horizon); Weather -0.086 [-0.115, -0.057] (overconfidence, the opposite direction, at short horizons, with slopes 0.69-0.97 within 48 hours); Crypto/Finance/Entertainment cluster near zero (well-calibrated). — [arXiv:2602.19520](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.19520v1), accessed 2026-09-18. (Preprint.)
- Same paper, trade-size effect: on Kalshi, large political trades (>100 contracts) produce a calibration slope of 1.74 vs 1.19 for single-contract trades — a gap of 0.53 [0.29, 0.75] — meaning large traders in Kalshi politics markets are associated with *more* mispricing/underconfidence at the point of their trade, not less. This effect "does not replicate on Polymarket" (Δ=+0.11 [-0.15, +0.39], not significant), pointing to a Kalshi-specific microstructure driver rather than a universal one. — [arXiv:2602.19520](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.19520v1), accessed 2026-09-18.
- Weather-specific: an independent analysis (CalibShi) of 8,494 settled Kalshi KXHIGHNY (NYC daily high temperature) markets found raw Expected Calibration Error (ECE) of 0.01624, reducible to 0.00109 (a "14.8x improvement") via isotonic-regression recalibration. — [CalibShi: Kalshi Weather Market Miscalibration Analysis](https://www.zerve.ai/gallery/85cce830-f612-4b23-8b78-34d7da65a2c6), accessed 2026-09-18. This is **not an academic/peer-reviewed source** (a data-science portfolio/gallery page); treat the ECE numbers as indicative, not authoritative, and note the page did not specify which price bands drove the miscalibration or whether it exceeds typical spread — that specific comparison could not be found.
- Near-expiry/end-of-contract distortion in sports (Kalshi NBA/MLB/NHL, ~23M trades): calibration parameters "deviate sharply as contract expiry approaches" — a power-law exponent of ~1.0 mid-contract rises to 1.27-1.31 in the final 10 minutes across leagues, and a Platt slope parameter reaches 1.62 (NBA) to 4.56 (NHL) near expiry, with contracts at p=0.4 showing near-zero empirical win rates in that window. — [arXiv:2607.14430](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14430v1), accessed 2026-09-18. This reads as evidence of a genuine near-expiry mispricing/overreaction pattern distinct from the long-horizon favourite-longshot bias, though the paper explicitly excludes fees from its reported figures.
- Weather-market implied-vs-realized bias: a separate SSRN working paper on weather hedging states "the implied-temperature bias (market-implied minus realized settlement value) is approximately zero," and that Kalshi's 8°F bin ladder means realized temperature falls outside the traded bin on "17% of days," with a "binarization cost of synthesizing a degree-day claim from binary bins... roughly 1% of exposure variance." — [Prediction Markets as Weather Hedges: Geographic Basis Risk, Settlement Constraints, and Contract Replication, Ziye Luo, SSRN 7138562](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/7138562.pdf?abstractid=7138562&mirid=1), surfaced via search-engine summary 2026-09-18; direct PDF fetch returned 403, so this is a **secondhand/search-summary citation, not independently verified against the primary text.**

### Inferences
- The literature does not support a single universal calibration bias direction — Weather is *overconfident* at short horizons (opposite sign to the classic favourite-longshot bias found in Politics/Sports at long horizons), which matters directly for the reader's weather-bracket dataset: it implies weather-specific dynamics may not generalize from the Politics/Sports favourite-longshot literature, and any weather-specific edge should be sized/tested independently rather than assumed to follow the same sign as political markets.
- The "implied-temperature bias ≈ zero" finding from the Ziye Luo weather-hedging paper is broadly consistent with the reader's own finding that a bias exists at the midpoint but is smaller than the spread — i.e., independent work also finds Kalshi weather markets close to unbiased in aggregate, with the residual bias small.
- The Kalshi-specific "large trades = more underconfidence in politics" finding suggests informed retail order flow does not straightforwardly correct the bias on Kalshi even at larger sizes — a point relevant to whether a solo trader can free-ride off larger, presumably more informed, participants.

### Gaps
- No paper found explicitly tests for weekend effects or round-number effects (e.g., prices clustering/mispricing at 50c, 25c, 75c strikes) in Kalshi or Polymarket data. This appears to be an open gap in the literature as of the search date — searches for "round number effect prediction markets" and "weekend effect Kalshi" did not surface dedicated academic treatment.
- Category-level calibration numbers for categories beyond Politics/Sports/Weather/Crypto/Finance/Entertainment (e.g., Kalshi's newer "Culture," "Climate," or one-off event categories) were not found.

## Documented profitable strategies: cross-venue arbitrage, calendar/ladder arbitrage, correlated contracts, post-news drift

### Takeaway
The literature documents real but rapidly-decaying cross-venue and information-diffusion mispricings — one estimate puts total arbitrage extraction at ~$40M over a year across the whole market (i.e., concentrated among a small number of fast participants) — but the one paper found that explicitly computes *executable* (buy-ask/sell-bid) returns on an underreaction/drift strategy finds those returns turn **negative** once the spread is applied, directly reinforcing the reader's own adverse-selection finding.

### Cited Findings
- NBA in-game drift (Kalshi win-probability contracts, 1,438 games, 2,876 team-level contracts, 409,512 contract-minute observations, April 2025-May 2026): the coefficient of contemporaneous price change on a play-by-play win-probability benchmark change is 0.638 (vs. 1.0 for full efficiency), i.e. prices underreact to news, with the underreaction gap correcting gradually over 5/10/15-minute windows (0.459 net-drift coefficient at 5 minutes). However: "executable-style returns that buy at the ask and sell at the bid are negative" — the underreaction is real but the bid-ask spread more than absorbs it, making it "economically untradeable in practice." The incomplete updating concentrates in "illiquid markets with salient events." — [When Do Markets Fully Process Public Information? Evidence from Real-Time Prediction Markets, arXiv:2606.07811](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.07811), accessed 2026-09-18. (Preprint.) This is the single most directly relevant academic finding to the reader's constraint: it is an independent, quantified confirmation that a real, statistically detectable pricing inefficiency (underreaction) does not survive the spread on Kalshi in a comparable (high-frequency, sports) setting.
- Cross-venue arbitrage extraction estimate (blog secondary source, citing on-chain/exchange data, not a peer-reviewed paper): "arbitrageurs extracted ~$40M from pricing mispricings (April 2024-April 2025)" across Kalshi/Polymarket, and "one bot extracted $271,500 in 30 days exploiting latency." — [Why You're Losing Money to Bots and Insiders on Kalshi, Turbine blog](https://www.turbinefi.com/blog/why-prediction-market-trades-get-picked-off-2026), accessed 2026-09-18. **Not an academic source** — treat as an industry estimate/anecdote, not a verified academic effect size; included because it is the only quantified cross-venue arbitrage total found, and it is directionally consistent with academic microstructure findings (adverse selection/toxic flow is concentrated among a small number of fast, well-capitalized participants).
- Parlay/correlated-contract mispricing (Kalshi NBA/MLB/NHL cross-game parlays, 15-day window April 29-May 13 2026): parlays are systematically overpriced relative to the product of leg prices, growing roughly "3% per additional leg," reaching 22-31% overpricing (ratio 1.22-1.31) at 10-11 legs — and this is stated *before* exchange fees are applied. This implies a documented, sizeable mispricing in a specific correlated-contract structure (parlays), though the paper's own framing is about overpricing relative to independence assumptions, not necessarily a fee-inclusive tradeable edge for someone selling into parlays. — [arXiv:2607.14430](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14430v1), accessed 2026-09-18.
- Thin-market mispricing persistence (blog/industry source, not verified as peer-reviewed): "persistent mispricings exist in low-liquidity contracts with a half-life of approximately 48 hours," decaying as arbitrage capital is deployed, with the claim that "in a thin market, a 5-cent mispricing can persist for days or even weeks because the capital required to correct it exceeds what anyone is willing to deploy." — [Prediction Market Pricing Efficiency: A Statistical Review, Quant Trader Journal](https://quanttraderjournal.com/articles/prediction-market-efficiency), surfaced via search 2026-09-18. **Not a verified academic source; specific "48-hour half-life" figure is unsourced within the piece itself and should be treated as an unverified claim**, not cited as an established literature result.
- Institutional liquidity entering prediction markets: a 2026 arXiv paper studies "what happens when institutional liquidity enters prediction markets" with an "identification, measurement, and synthetic proof of concept" framing, implying at least one paper directly models the effect of larger capital entering thin markets — but full-text detail was not fetched in this pass (would need a follow-up fetch of the PDF to extract effect sizes). — [arXiv:2604.10005](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.10005), noted from search results 2026-09-18, **not fetched/verified — flag as a gap** (see below).

### Inferences
- The strongest, most rigorous evidence bearing directly on "does a documented strategy survive the spread" is the NBA drift paper's negative result — this is the closest published analogue to the reader's own weather-bracket finding, and it independently corroborates the broader pattern that Kalshi's realized bid-ask spread is wide enough to eat measurable, statistically real mispricings across at least two different market categories (weather brackets per the reader's own work; sports win-probability drift per this paper).
- Where a real edge does appear to survive in the literature, it is concentrated among fast/large/technically sophisticated participants (bots exploiting latency, a small number of arbitrageurs capturing the bulk of the ~$40M industry-wide estimate) — not a strategy type accessible to a solo retail trader running passive or moderately-latent execution.
- Parlay overpricing is the one number in this section that plausibly represents a genuine, sizeable (>20% at high leg counts), structurally-explainable mispricing rather than a noisy microstructure artifact — but no paper reviewed backtests actually selling into overpriced parlays net of fees and correlation risk, so it remains a documented pattern rather than a demonstrated profitable strategy.

### Gaps
- No paper found directly backtests calendar/ladder arbitrage within a single event's bracket structure (e.g., across Kalshi's temperature brackets or multiple strike levels of the same underlying) net of costs — the "Unravelling the Probabilistic Forest" paper (arXiv:2508.03474) appeared highly relevant by title (arbitrage in prediction markets, with appendix sections on "Bids Statistics and Delta Measurement" and "Profit on the Dollar Difference Between Strategies") but its PDF could not be parsed as text in this session (returned mostly binary/metadata); this is a real gap — a follow-up fetch/OCR of that specific paper is recommended before treating ladder arbitrage as unaddressed in the literature.
- The institutional-liquidity paper (arXiv:2604.10005) was identified but not read in full; its findings on how large capital entry affects thin-market pricing are an open gap.
- No rigorous, peer-reviewed, cost-inclusive backtest of Kalshi-vs-Polymarket-vs-sportsbook cross-venue arbitrage was found; only an industry-blog aggregate dollar estimate ($40M/year) exists, and that figure's methodology could not be verified.

## Adverse selection and market making in thin event markets

### Takeaway
The most directly relevant academic paper (Bartlett & O'Hara, Stanford Law/SSRN, 41.6M Kalshi trades) finds that market makers in single-name (thin, idiosyncratic) markets face roughly *twice* the adverse-selection cost of makers in broad-based markets, but are compensated by an even larger behavioral overpricing of YES by retail — a "cross-subsidy" that makes maker economics work in aggregate, but only because retail systematically overpays; nothing in the paper suggests this cross-subsidy is reliably capturable by a small, non-professional maker without institutional-grade quoting infrastructure.

### Cited Findings
- Bartlett & O'Hara, "Adverse Selection in Prediction Markets: Evidence from Kalshi" (SSRN 6615739, April 2026), 41.6 million Kalshi trades: using Kyle's λ and a Glosten-Harris-style spread decomposition, "single-name markets exhibit greater informed price impact than broad-based markets... effective spreads are only modestly wider, and market makers earn twice as much per contract" in single-name markets — a "puzzle resolved by the finding that traders systematically overbet YES in markets that predominantly settle NO, generating a behavioral surplus that cross-subsidizes adverse selection." Using an adapted VPIN toxicity metric, "one-sided order flow predicts maker losses in single-name markets but not broad-based markets." — [SSRN abstract, surfaced via Stanford Law press page and search](https://law.stanford.edu/press/adverse-selection-in-prediction-markets-evidence-from-kalshi/) and [SSRN 6615739](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6615739), accessed 2026-09-18. Full PDF fetch returned 403 both directly and via Delivery.cfm; **findings are from the abstract/press-summary level only, not independently verified against full text or tables.**
- A secondary summary of the same paper (via industry blog, treat as secondary and lower-confidence than the abstract-level claims above): "single-name contracts show 'roughly twice as severe' adverse selection compared to broad-based markets. Market makers widen spreads by approximately 2x in single-name contracts to compensate for informed flow." — [Turbine blog](https://www.turbinefi.com/blog/why-prediction-market-trades-get-picked-off-2026), accessed 2026-09-18. Consistent with, but not an independent replication of, the SSRN abstract.
- Polymarket order-book microstructure (600 markets, 28-day window Feb 28-Mar 27 2026, 30.3B WebSocket events, 255M on-chain trades over 52 days): quoted half-spreads are strongly probability-dependent — "markets trading below 0.10 probability exhibit spreads of 1,300-1,800 basis points, while central-range markets (0.4-0.6 probability) show approximately 400 basis points," versus single-digit-bps norms in modern equities. Despite this, "the median adverse-selection coefficient across the top-100 stratum is essentially zero in probability points," with wide spreads attributed to "liquidity provision constraints rather than information asymmetries" — i.e., on Polymarket specifically, spread width in this sample is explained more by thin/costly liquidity provision than by informed-trader adverse selection. Maker concentration: median Herfindahl index 0.031 (~32 effective makers per market), i.e. liquidity provision is fragmented rather than dominated by a few professional makers. — [The Anatomy of a Decentralized Prediction Market: Microstructure Evidence from the Polymarket Order Book, arXiv:2604.24366](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.24366v1), accessed 2026-09-18. (Preprint.)
- General microstructure framing: "the market microstructure tradition stemming from Kyle (1985) and Glosten and Milgrom (1985)... [applies], with the core insight that bid-ask spreads compensate market makers for adverse-selection risk against informed trades," but prediction markets have two complications relative to standard asset microstructure: they "resolve at known terminal states, bounding the informed trader's information edge in time," and "the relevant information is often event-specific rather than fundamental." — summarized from search results referencing this theoretical framing in the context of the papers above, accessed 2026-09-18 (this is a synthesis/paraphrase surfaced by the search tool rather than a single directly-quotable primary source; treat as background theory, not a new empirical finding).

### Inferences
- The Bartlett & O'Hara vs. Polymarket order-book papers appear to disagree on *why* spreads are wide/makers are compensated: Bartlett & O'Hara (Kalshi) attribute maker profitability to a behavioral retail overpricing cross-subsidy that offsets genuine adverse selection; the Polymarket order-book paper attributes wide spreads mainly to liquidity-provision costs, finding near-zero adverse-selection coefficients. This is a genuine cross-venue discrepancy worth flagging rather than resolving — it may reflect real structural differences between Kalshi (CLOB, regulated, US-retail-heavy) and Polymarket (AMM/crypto-native, different trader base), or differing methodologies (VPIN/Kyle's λ vs. spread decomposition).
- For the reader's specific finding (passive selling at the ask is perfectly adversely selected — 100% of eventual winners fill vs 26.8% of losers), the Bartlett & O'Hara single-name/broad-based distinction is directly relevant: weather brackets are single-name-like (idiosyncratic, thin, retail-heavy) markets, exactly the category the paper finds has 2x the adverse selection of broad/index-like markets. This is consistent with — and gives independent academic grounding to — treating weather brackets as a high-adverse-selection category by construction, not a market-specific fluke.
- Neither paper found gives a clean answer to "is passive quoting viable for a small participant" — Bartlett & O'Hara's cross-subsidy mechanism implies profitability exists at the *aggregate* maker level (across all makers, weighted by size/sophistication), not necessarily for an individual small maker who cannot control adverse selection exposure the way large, diversified makers can (e.g., via multi-market inventory netting, faster requoting, or informational advantages).

### Gaps
- No paper found reports maker profitability broken out by maker size/capital (i.e., whether the "makers earn twice as much per contract" finding holds specifically for small, undiversified makers or only for large ones who can average across many markets).
- Could not independently verify Bartlett & O'Hara's tables/tests beyond the abstract due to repeated 403s on both the direct SSRN and Delivery.cfm URLs — a logged-in SSRN session or an alternate mirror (e.g., ResearchGate, which was found: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/404044013) would be needed for full verification; this should be treated as the single highest-priority follow-up read given how central it is to the "adverse selection in thin markets" question.

## Where inefficiency concentrates (volume, category, time, contract age)

### Takeaway
The literature and CFTC data together point to inefficiency concentrating in: single-name/idiosyncratic (vs. broad-based) markets, low-volume/thin contracts generally, the near-expiry window in fast-moving sports markets specifically (a distinct phenomenon from the long-horizon favourite-longshot bias), and — per Kalshi's own volume data — a market dominated by sports (≈87% of Kalshi's trailing-year volume), meaning "thin" markets are the categorical norm rather than the exception outside of sports/politics/crypto headliners.

### Cited Findings
- Category volume concentration: "Sports dominates the larger venue, taking roughly 87% of Kalshi's $39.7 billion trailing-year volume as of February 2026." — [via CNBC/industry summary surfaced in search](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/02/prediction-markets-mostly-have-thinly-traded-contracts-.html) and related 2026 volume-statistics pieces, accessed 2026-09-18. This is a data/industry-journalism source, not academic, but is a direct, checkable volume statistic rather than a modeled claim.
- CFTC filing data on contract proliferation: "Between 2006 and 2020, DCMs listed an average of five new event contracts each year. In 2021, that figure rose to 131. New listings remained around that level until 2025, when they again spiked to approximately 1,600." — [Federal Register, Prediction Markets NPRM, 2026-05105](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/03/16/2026-05105/prediction-markets), accessed 2026-09-18. This is a primary regulatory-filing source and directly supports the inference that a large and growing share of listed contracts are necessarily new/thin (low trading history) at any given time, consistent with "newly listed contracts are a locus of inefficiency" even though no paper directly quantifies mispricing-by-contract-age.
- Bartlett & O'Hara's single-name vs. broad-based distinction (see above) is the clearest peer-reviewed-adjacent statement on where adverse-selection-driven mispricing concentrates: single-name (idiosyncratic, typically lower-volume) markets show ~2x the informed price impact and toxicity signal of broad-based markets. — [SSRN 6615739](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6615739), accessed 2026-09-18 (abstract-level, see caveats above).
- Near-expiry concentration in sports specifically: the extreme miscalibration documented in arXiv:2607.14430 is explicitly a "final 10 minutes" phenomenon (Platt slope 1.62-4.56) distinct from mid-contract calibration (slope ~1.0), giving a concrete, quantified example of a specific time-window where mispricing concentrates. — [arXiv:2607.14430](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14430v1), accessed 2026-09-18.
- Trade-size / large-trader effect concentrating in Kalshi politics specifically (not Polymarket, not other Kalshi categories tested): see the 0.53 calibration-slope gap for large vs. small political trades cited above. — [arXiv:2602.19520](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.19520v1), accessed 2026-09-18.

### Inferences
- Combining the volume-concentration statistic (87% of Kalshi volume in sports) with the single-name/broad-based adverse-selection finding suggests that, by count of markets (though not by volume), the *majority* of Kalshi's listed markets outside the sports/politics/crypto headline categories are exactly the thin, single-name, high-adverse-selection type the literature flags as worst for passive liquidity provision — weather brackets fit this description structurally.
- The "newly listed contracts" question is answered only indirectly (proliferation stats + general thin-market liquidity mechanics from non-academic sources); no academic paper was found that directly measures mispricing as a function of contract age/days-since-listing.

### Gaps
- No academic paper found directly measures "mispricing magnitude vs. contract age since listing" as a controlled variable — this is inferred rather than directly evidenced.
- No breakdown found of where retail-heavy order flow specifically concentrates by time-of-day (the reader's question about "specific times of day" is unanswered in the literature located this session).

## Published work on weather derivatives / temperature forecasting markets specifically

### Takeaway
Academic coverage of exchange-traded temperature/weather event contracts specifically (as opposed to legacy OTC CME-style weather derivatives) is thin — one directly relevant SSRN working paper on Kalshi weather-as-hedge exists, plus the domain-specific calibration paper's Weather category results and one non-academic (data-science portfolio) miscalibration analysis; no dedicated peer-reviewed study of tradeable weather-bracket mispricing net of costs was found.

### Cited Findings
- "Prediction Markets as Weather Hedges: Geographic Basis Risk, Settlement Constraints, and Contract Replication" (Ziye Luo, SSRN 7138562): finds implied-vs-realized temperature bias "approximately zero" in aggregate; documents that realized temperature falls outside Kalshi's 8°F settlement bin on "17% of days," and quantifies the cost of synthesizing a continuous degree-day exposure from discrete binary bins at "roughly 1% of exposure variance." — [SSRN 7138562](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/7138562.pdf?abstractid=7138562&mirid=1), surfaced via search-summary 2026-09-18; **full text not independently fetched (403), findings are secondhand from search-engine summarization of the abstract/paper and should be treated with corresponding caution.**
- Domain-specific calibration paper's Weather-category finding: short-horizon *overconfidence* (domain intercept -0.086 [-0.115, -0.057], slopes 0.69-0.97 within 48 hours) — the opposite sign from Politics/Sports long-horizon underconfidence — meaning Weather-category contracts are, if anything, priced too close to 0/100 relative to true probability at short (within-48-hour) horizons, not compressed toward 50/50 the way the classic favourite-longshot bias would predict. — [arXiv:2602.19520](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.19520v1), accessed 2026-09-18.
- Non-academic CalibShi analysis of 8,494 Kalshi KXHIGHNY (NYC daily high) settled markets found ECE of 0.01624 pre-correction, 0.00109 post-isotonic-recalibration — see full caveat above; this source did not specify which price bands (e.g., specifically the 5-10c longshot band the reader studied) drove the raw miscalibration, so it neither confirms nor contradicts the reader's specific 4.9%-settle-rate finding, but is broadly consistent with "some correctable miscalibration exists but it's not huge in absolute ECE terms."

### Inferences
- The overconfidence-at-short-horizon finding for Weather (arXiv:2602.19520) combined with the reader's own finding (a favourite-longshot-style bias exists in weather brackets, longshots priced 5-10c settle YES 4.9% — i.e. somewhat overpriced relative to a "fair" 5-10c band, which is a form of longshot overpricing/compression-toward-uncertainty at least at that specific price band) suggests these two findings are not strictly contradictory: the academic paper's headline "overconfidence" sign is about the *domain average* calibration slope across all price levels and short horizons broadly, whereas the reader's finding is specifically about the low-probability tail. Aggregate domain-level slope and tail-specific behavior can differ, so this is not a true conflict, but it does mean the domain-level academic finding cannot be used to directly validate or invalidate the reader's tail-specific result — they are answering different questions.
- The near-zero aggregate implied-temperature bias (Ziye Luo paper) is consistent with the reader's finding that the longshot bias, while real, is small relative to the spread — both point toward Kalshi weather markets being close to efficient/unbiased in aggregate, with any exploitable mispricing concentrated in specific corners (the tail, per the reader's work) rather than pervasive.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or working paper was found that runs a cost-inclusive backtest specifically on Kalshi weather-bracket trading strategies (taker or maker side). The reader's own 9,252-market empirical analysis appears to be more granular on this specific question (bracket-level, price-band-level, fill-side-conditional) than anything found in the published/preprint literature as of this search.
- No published work was found on Polymarket-hosted weather/climate contracts specifically (Polymarket's weather category volume/liquidity appears much smaller than Kalshi's, and no dedicated study surfaced).
- CFTC comment-letter text specifically discussing weather/event-contract market quality (as opposed to general prediction-market policy) was not found in this search pass — the CFTC ANPRM/NPRM documents found are about prediction markets broadly (manipulation, insider information, "enumerated activities") rather than weather contracts specifically.

## Overall synthesis for a solo Kalshi trader (beyond the reader's existing weather-bracket work)

### Takeaway
The 2023-2026 literature converges on a consistent, sobering picture for a solo trader: real, statistically significant mispricings exist and are documented across categories (favourite-longshot bias, near-expiry sports miscalibration, parlay overpricing, cross-venue gaps, in-game underreaction), but every instance where a paper explicitly computes cost-inclusive/executable returns finds the edge is destroyed by the bid-ask spread, and the residual profitable extraction that does exist in the ecosystem (the ~$40M/year cross-venue figure, latency-arbitrage bots) is concentrated among fast, well-capitalized, technically sophisticated participants — not a strategy shape accessible to passive or moderate-latency solo trading. The clearest "adds beyond the reader's own work" contributions are: (1) an independent academic confirmation (NBA drift paper) that a *different* market category also shows real inefficiency destroyed by spread, generalizing the reader's own weather finding; (2) the Bartlett & O'Hara single-name-vs-broad-based framework, which gives an academic label and comparative magnitude (~2x) to the specific type of thin/idiosyncratic market (like weather brackets) the reader is already trading, and confirms this category is structurally the worst for adverse selection; and (3) parlay overpricing (22-31% at 10+ legs) as a documented, sizeable, distinct mispricing type the reader has not yet investigated, worth flagging as a candidate for future research even though no cost-inclusive backtest of it yet exists in the literature.

(see above sections — this is a synthesis, not new findings)

### Inferences
- Given that the one rigorous cost-inclusive test found (arXiv:2606.07811, NBA drift) reaches the same qualitative conclusion as the reader's own weather work (real edge, destroyed by spread), the prior should shift further toward: on Kalshi, *documented statistical mispricing surviving the spread* is rare-to-absent in the categories studied so far in the academic literature, and the burden of proof for any new claimed strategy should be a full cost-inclusive backtest, not just a raw-price calibration finding.
- Parlay overpricing is the one candidate in this research pass that (a) is large in magnitude, (b) is stated to exist even after accounting for the products-of-legs benchmark, and (c) has not yet been tested net of fees/correlation-risk in any paper found — it is the most promising single lead for further investigation, with the caveat that correlation/hedging complexity for cross-game parlays could easily eat the apparent edge in practice.

### Gaps
- No unified cost model (fees + spread + adverse selection jointly) was found applied to any of the "documented" strategies above except the NBA drift paper — most papers report raw/mid-price mispricing and note fees are excluded, leaving the true tradeable-edge question open for cross-venue arbitrage, parlay overpricing, and the long-horizon favourite-longshot bias.
