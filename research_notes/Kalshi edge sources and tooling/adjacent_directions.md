# Adjacent Directions for a Prediction-Market Research Project (After Trading Strategies Failed to Find an Edge)

## Which event-contract families plausibly still have retail-accessible edges in 2026 (weekly econ releases, Fed vs futures, CPI vs nowcasts, niche/low-volume)?

### Takeaway
The best-documented edge in Kalshi's economics category is Fed-decision markets beating CME Fed funds futures in the day(s) immediately before FOMC meetings, but this appears to be a regime-dependent artifact of how the effective fed funds rate (EFFR) behaved in 2022–2025, and evidence suggests it has degraded since the EFFR started drifting intra-month in September 2025 — i.e., a documented edge that may already be closing. CPI/jobless-claims/mortgage-rate markets have no published evidence of a retail-exploitable edge; the evidence that does exist (Kalshi's own calibration study, a Fed working paper) is about calibration/accuracy of the platform as a forecasting tool, not about profitable mispricing that survives the spread and fees.

### Cited Findings
- A Federal Reserve study found Kalshi's Fed funds rate forecasts, particularly the day before FOMC meetings, had a "perfect forecast record" on the most likely outcome and a lower forecast error than Fed funds futures in the run-up to meetings — [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/jasonbrett/2026/02/23/kalshi-polymarket-offer-evolution-of-predictions-for-fed-wall-street/) (accessed 2026-09-18); also referenced via [DeFi Rate](https://defirate.com/news/federal-reserve-study-finds-kalshi-markets-rival-traditional-economic-forecast-tools/) (accessed 2026-09-18)
- A public GitHub backtest of the Kalshi/Polymarket-vs-CME fed-funds-futures basis found "the edge is real in the 2022-2025 'pinned' regime and is not distinguishable from noise in the regime that began in September 2025," because "the effective fed funds rate used to hold one value all month, and now it moves inside the month, which is exactly what the hedge cannot absorb" — [GitHub: fed-basis-backtest](https://github.com/YichengYang-Ethan/fed-basis-backtest) (accessed 2026-09-18)
- Kalshi's own research, using the complete history of 2,243,741 resolved markets across eleven categories (2021–mid-2026), found prices "extremely well calibrated" as markets approach resolution, with elections/politics/finance showing the strongest calibration; markets with as little as ~$10,000 volume or 20 traders still produced useful forecasts, and the study found "no evidence of significant distortion in any direction" — [Kalshi Research: Calibration in Prediction Markets](https://news.kalshi.com/p/prediction-market-calibration-how-accurate-is-kalshi) (accessed 2026-09-18)
- Kalshi's economics category covers CPI, PPI, GDP, nonfarm payrolls, Fed-funds decisions, unemployment rate, and jobless claims; employment-related events (jobless claims, layoffs, unemployment) are described as the most numerous economics contracts on the platform, and gas-price markets have drawn notably high volume within the energy/economics category — [OddsShopper: Kalshi Economic Markets](https://www.oddsshopper.com/articles/prediction-markets/kalshi-economic-markets) and [Barchart](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/34444170/markets-bet-on-whether-average-gas-prices-will-be-above-3-20) (accessed 2026-09-18)
- Sports markets account for more than 80% of total Kalshi trading volume, implying economics/finance/weather categories are comparatively thin — [DeFi Rate: Kalshi Volume](https://defirate.com/prediction-markets/volume/kalshi/) (accessed 2026-09-18). Note: sports/politics/culture categories are geofenced off in Washington State per the user's constraints, which rules out the highest-volume category entirely and pushes any viable edge into the thinner economics/finance/weather categories.
- A CESifo/UCD/CEPR working paper ("Makers and Takers: The Economics of the Kalshi Prediction Market," Bürgi, Deng & Whelan, 2026) using transaction-level data on over 300,000 contracts finds that makers (passive quoters) earn higher returns than takers, but both maker and taker returns exhibit a favorite–longshot pattern, and the pattern can be reproduced by a model with only "modest levels of disagreement and a small tendency to overstate small probabilities" — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5502658), [CESifo/econstor PDF](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/338324/1/cesifo1_wp12122.pdf) (accessed 2026-09-18)
- A public replication project asks whether the Bürgi/Deng/Whelan maker-return advantage and favorite-longshot pattern "survive maker fees and public documentation," i.e., whether the edge has already been arbitraged away since publication — [GitHub: kalshi-makers-takers-persistence](https://github.com/Vladosyna/kalshi-makers-takers-persistence) (accessed 2026-09-18; repo description read only, replication results not independently verified in this research pass)

### Inferences
- The maker-earns-more-than-taker finding in Bürgi/Deng/Whelan is in tension with the user's own passive-quoting result ("passive quoting is perfectly adversely selected"). This is worth investigating directly rather than taking either result at face value — the discrepancy could be explained by market/category selection (their sample spans 2021–2025 across all categories; the user's test may have been on a narrower or more recent slice), by the maker-fee changes Kalshi introduced, or by execution/latency differences. This is a natural, low-cost next step given the existing paper-broker and event-clustered stats infrastructure: replicate Bürgi/Deng/Whelan's maker-return finding on the user's own data and reconcile it with their adverse-selection finding.
- The FOMC/Fed-funds-futures basis is the single most concretely evidenced "edge" surfaced in this research, but it (a) requires holding/hedging against CME futures, which is a materially different and more complex trade than a standalone Kalshi position, and (b) the public evidence says the edge specifically degraded in the current (Sept 2025+) regime — i.e., it is not a live, ready-to-harvest edge as of today's date without new work to determine if/how it persists in the new regime.
- No source found supports an accessible, still-live edge in CPI, jobless claims, mortgage rates, or gas-price markets specifically. The absence of such published findings is itself informative given how much retail and academic attention Kalshi's economics markets have received — if a clean edge existed and were public, it likely would have surfaced in this search.

### Gaps
- No direct data was found on typical bid-ask spreads or trading volume for jobless-claims, mortgage-rate, or gas-price contracts specifically (as opposed to CPI/Fed, which have more coverage), so it is not possible to say how "thin" or exploitable these niche series currently are.
- No source quantified how many minutes/hours it typically takes Kalshi CPI or jobless-claims markets to fully reprice after the BLS/DOL release, which is the sharpest version of key question 1 ("how fast do markets incorporate it"). This would require an original event-study using the release-time data pipeline the user already has.

## Intraday/in-event strategies: is there evidence event-contract markets are slower to reprice during an event, and what is needed to test this honestly?

### Takeaway
Yes — there is strong, directly relevant, methodologically careful academic evidence that Kalshi NBA markets specifically underreact to public information during live play, especially in illiquid conditions and "clutch" late-game states, but the same paper shows the predictable drift this creates is absorbed by the bid-ask spread and is not a live arbitrage once execution costs are included. This is close to a direct answer for the user's own NBA series and is worth reading as a check on/companion to their existing NBA backtest.

### Cited Findings
- Angelini & De Angelis (June 2026), "When Do Markets Fully Process Public Information? Evidence from Real-Time Prediction Markets," uses 409,512 Kalshi NBA contract-minute observations across 1,438 games (April 2025–May 2026) merged with NBA play-by-play data. Relative to an out-of-sample benchmark win-probability model (excluding the Kalshi price itself), "a one-minute change in the benchmark probability is associated with only about a 0.64-for-one contemporaneous change in the Kalshi midpoint" — i.e., prices underreact to public signals on impact — [arXiv:2606.07811](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.07811) (accessed 2026-09-18)
- The underreaction is not uniform: the coefficient (β, ideally =1 under efficiency) is 0.700 in non-clutch 4th-quarter minutes but falls to 0.509 in "clutch time" (final 5 minutes, score margin ≤5 points) — i.e., prices are least efficient exactly when public signals are most decision-relevant. Across quarters it otherwise ranges 0.620–0.673. — same source
- Illiquidity is the key driver of incomplete updating: salient events (three-point shots, lead changes, scoring runs) are incorporated quickly when markets are liquid, but the same salient signals produce larger underreaction when liquidity is low (interaction term positive and significant, p<0.01 level) — same source
- The missing adjustment predicts future price drift over the next 1–15 minutes (coefficients 0.15–0.24 raw, 0.38–0.48 net of future benchmark changes, all significant at 1%) — i.e., there is genuine gradual price discovery, not just noise — same source
- Critically, the paper directly tests whether this drift is a tradeable edge: "executable-style returns that buy at the ask and sell at the bid are negative" even for large mispricing gaps (e.g., -0.92% to -1.20% for the full sample vs. +0.39%-0.87% midpoint returns), so "the predictable midpoint drift is largely absorbed by trading costs" — same source
- The paper's methodology for testing this honestly: benchmark win probability is estimated out-of-sample via five-fold cross-fitting at the game level (never trained on data from the same game being predicted), excludes the contemporaneous Kalshi price from the benchmark to avoid circularity, and is robustness-checked against five alternative benchmark specifications and multiple quote-quality/liquidity subsamples — same source
- Related literature cited in that paper: Croxson & Reade (2014) find rapid and full adjustment around goals scored near soccer half-time; Gauriot & Page (2026, Economic Journal) find "mostly rapid adjustment, with short-lived underreaction to large shocks" in binary options markets around knife-edge information shocks; Angelini, De Angelis & Singleton (2022, International Journal of Forecasting) document both mispricing and behavioral patterns in in-play prediction markets — cited within [arXiv:2606.07811](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.07811), original papers not independently fetched in this pass
- General market commentary (non-academic) claims "during live play, market prices lag reality by 10-30 seconds after score changes" and that "calibration breaks down during live events, with pre-game prices being more accurate than in-game prices" — [search-engine synthesis, original source not identified](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.03645); this specific 10-30 second figure could not be traced to a primary source and should be treated as unverified.

### Inferences
- Because the user's own NBA model already "loses to the market and to plain ELO," the Angelini/De Angelis finding suggests the exploitable inefficiency in Kalshi NBA markets is not in pre-game or slow-moving fundamentals (where the user has already tested and failed) but specifically in the live, in-play, high-frequency reaction window — and specifically in illiquid, clutch-time moments. However, the same paper's finding that executable returns are negative after spread costs is a strong warning: this is very likely a "real but not tradeable at retail" effect once realistic fees and the bid-ask spread (which the user's backtester already models) are applied. A rigorous replication using their own fee model and no-look-ahead backtester would either (a) confirm the effect is real but unprofitable for a solo retail participant post-costs, closing this avenue honestly, or (b) find retail-specific niches (e.g., certain leagues/markets with wider spreads where being the counterparty to the underreaction is profitable) — but the a priori expectation, following the published paper, should be (a).
- For weather markets specifically, no equivalent event-study evidence was found (the user's own framing that "weather markets after the day's high is effectively locked in" is untested by any source found here) — this remains the user's original hypothesis, not corroborated or refuted by outside literature. A weather-specific version of the Angelini/De Angelis methodology (benchmark truth model excluding contemporaneous price, cross-fitted, tested against executable returns) would be the honest way to test it, reusing the user's existing no-look-ahead backtester.
- To test any "markets are slow to reprice during live events" hypothesis honestly, the key ingredients evidenced by this paper are: (1) a benchmark "true" probability model built only from information available at each timestamp and never from the market price itself, ideally cross-fit out-of-sample; (2) high-frequency (sub-minute to minute-level) quote data with both bid and ask, not just a synthetic midpoint; (3) an executable-return test (buy-ask/sell-bid) as the final honesty check, since midpoint returns alone are known to overstate the opportunity; (4) clustering standard errors by event/game, which the user's infrastructure already does.

### Gaps
- No equivalent published in-play efficiency study was found for Kalshi weather, election-night, or economic-release markets — only NBA and (briefly) generic sports betting and binary options literature. The finding that in-play NBA markets underreact does not necessarily generalize to markets with a single resolving public data release (like weather's daily high) rather than continuous play-by-play information flow.
- No data was found on how large the illiquidity-driven mispricing needs to be, or what minimum size/liquidity threshold a market needs, before a solo retail trader (not a market maker with rebates) could plausibly profit net of Kalshi's real fee schedule — this is exactly the kind of question the user's existing fee-modelling and paper-broker infrastructure could answer directly with an in-play replication, but no external source quantifies it.

## Data-advantage plays that don't require speed: alternative data, and structural knowledge of settlement rules

### Takeaway
There is one concrete, well-documented Kalshi mechanical quirk (fee rounding on small orders) and general guidance that settlement-source and rounding/timezone details are "the ground truth a mispricing is measured against," but no source in this research surfaced a specific, currently-exploitable settlement-source mispricing case study (e.g., a real historical instance where a market resolved unexpectedly due to a rounding or timezone convention traders missed). This direction is plausible in principle but currently evidenced only by mechanism, not by a documented past profitable instance.

### Cited Findings
- Kalshi's general trading fee formula is fee = 0.07 × C × P × (1−P), rounded UP to the next cent; because of the P×(1−P) term, the fee peaks at a 50-cent price and shrinks toward the 1-cent and 99-cent extremes. On very small orders (1–2 contracts), this rounding can "meaningfully raise your effective rate" because a fractional-cent fee (e.g., $0.0175) still rounds up to a full cent; placing many small orders instead of consolidating into one larger order means "you pay the rounding penalty repeatedly" — [Allium: Kalshi Fees](https://www.allium.so/blog/kalshi-fees-why-the-cost-peaks-near-50-cents/) (accessed 2026-09-18); consistent framing at [OddsShopper: The Coin Flip Is The Most Expensive Trade](https://www.oddsshopper.com/articles/prediction-markets/kalshi-fees) (accessed 2026-09-18)
- On settlement mechanics generally: "the settlement source decides the outcome... what price gets compared to that strike when the window closes... is the ground truth a mispricing is measured against" — one source describes Kalshi's Bitcoin settlement using the CF Benchmarks real-time index with 60-second averaging and calls out unspecified "edge cases" in that methodology — [PredictionMarketsPicks: How Kalshi Settles Bitcoin](https://predictionmarketspicks.com/articles/how-kalshi-settles-bitcoin) (accessed 2026-09-18). Note: this source is a secondary/aggregator site, not Kalshi's own rulebook, and its specific "edge case" claims were not independently verified against Kalshi's official settlement documentation in this pass.
- Kalshi maintains a "Mispricing Scanner" tool marketed to find "mispriced binary markets," implying enough retail interest in structural mispricing that at least one third-party tool exists to scan for it — [PredictionMarketsPicks: Mispricing Scanner](https://predictionmarketspicks.com/tools/mispricing-scanner) (accessed 2026-09-18); this is a product listing, not independent evidence that the tool finds real, persistent edges.
- Kalshi has adjusted its "trader error" rules as it courts institutional traders, suggesting the platform is actively tightening the kinds of settlement/execution quirks that once could be exploited — [Yahoo Sports/Kalshi Tweaks Trader Error Rules](https://sports.yahoo.com/article/kalshi-tweaks-trader-error-rules-180921802.html) (accessed 2026-09-18)

### Inferences
- The fee-rounding quirk is real but is an execution-cost optimization (batch orders, avoid tiny fills), not a source of directional edge — it affects the user's own trading costs, not a mispricing to trade against a counterparty.
- The general framing ("settlement source is the ground truth") suggests the productive research direction is not "find a documented public case" (none surfaced) but to systematically audit, for every series the user already has data pipelines for (weather, economic releases), the exact wording of Kalshi's settlement source, rounding convention, and timezone cutoff against the underlying public data feed's own conventions — e.g., does the NWS or FRED source Kalshi cites round differently than Kalshi's own contract strike; does a "by 11:59pm ET" cutoff create an edge around data revised/released near a cutoff. This is unglamorous, low-volume, structural work that plays directly to the "solid, reusable infrastructure" the user already has (data pipelines with no-look-ahead guards) but was not the subject of any single case-study source found in this research.

### Gaps
- No historical case study was found of a retail trader profiting from a specific Kalshi settlement-source/rounding/timezone quirk (e.g., a market that resolved against the "obvious" outcome due to fine print). This entire key question is more speculative-but-plausible than evidenced; it would need to be tested directly against Kalshi's own published rulebooks per series, which is a document-reading exercise rather than something further web research can resolve.

## Quantitative research careers: what artifacts are actually valued, and what separates a credible research portfolio from a toy bot?

### Takeaway
Hiring-oriented sources converge on: reproducible, well-documented research code that demonstrates methodological rigor (backtesting discipline, risk reasoning, no data leakage) matters more than a live trading track record or headline returns; a credible portfolio also explicitly separates "quant researcher" from "quant developer" skill demonstrations rather than blurring them, and is explicit about the limitations of what it found — all things the user's project already does (no-look-ahead guards, honest "the model loses" writeups) but that a "toy bot" pitched as a moneymaker typically does not.

### Cited Findings
- Reviewed example quant portfolios on GitHub emphasize "how you structure research, reason about risk, and build reproducible analysis code," covering systematic trading, market-regime modeling, portfolio risk, derivatives, financial ML, with "production-oriented Python architecture with benchmarking, visualization, and backtesting" — [GitHub: jmiaie/quant-research-portfolio](https://github.com/jmiaie/quant-research-portfolio) (accessed 2026-09-18)
- Portfolios are advised to "demonstrate research quality without exposing proprietary trading rules, live integrations, credentials, or production execution details," and to sequence projects so they clearly address "both sides of a quant firm's hiring surface" — treating Quant Researcher (QR) and Quant Developer (QD) as "distinct roles with distinct skill demands, rather than blurring them" — [GitHub: berryO307/quant-portfolio](https://github.com/berryO307/quant-portfolio) (accessed 2026-09-18)
- Separately, general (non-Kalshi-specific) guidance on evaluating trading-bot/signal credibility states that legitimate research is "honest about their limitations" and that professional research shops "employ hundreds of researchers running thousands of experiments to find new signals, with most experiments failing and successful ones producing small, temporary edges that need constant refreshing" — framing that implies a documented negative result (an honestly-reported failed edge search) is itself evidence of realistic research practice, not a failure to have anything to show — [Quantt: Best AI Trading Bot guide](https://www.quantt.co.uk/resources/best-ai-trading-bot-guide) (accessed 2026-09-18). This source is consumer-facing rather than a hiring-manager perspective and should be weighted accordingly.

### Inferences
- By the standards actually cited (reproducibility, explicit risk/limitation reasoning, methodological rigor over headline returns, no data leakage), the user's existing project is already closer to a credible research artifact than a toy bot: it has a backtester with explicit no-look-ahead guards, real fee modelling, event-clustered statistics (a specific, correct econometric practice for this kind of data), and a git history of honestly reported negative results. The main gap versus the cited portfolio guidance is presentation and packaging — the cited advice about separating QR-style research demonstrations from QD-style systems/infra demonstrations, and about sanitizing away anything that looks like a "wanted to make money and didn't" narrative in favor of "here is a rigorous test of an efficient-markets-adjacent hypothesis and here is what I found," suggests the highest-value non-trading deliverable is a cleaned-up public writeup/repo explicitly framed as market-efficiency research (citing, e.g., the Angelini/De Angelis and Bürgi/Deng/Whelan papers found in this research as academic peers) rather than as a failed trading bot.
- No source directly named which specific artifacts (e.g., a Sharpe ratio, a specific backtest visualization, a particular kind of writeup) are weighted most heavily by real hiring managers at quant trading firms; the search surfaced portfolio-construction advice from aspiring-candidate-authored repos, not primary statements from hiring managers themselves. This is a meaningful gap: the "toy bot vs. credible portfolio" distinction is well-evidenced qualitatively but not backed by primary hiring-manager sourcing in this pass.

### Gaps
- No primary source from an actual quant hiring manager, recruiter, or firm engineering blog was found and read in this research pass (search results returned candidate-authored portfolio repos and consumer bot-evaluation content, not firm-side hiring guidance). This key question is under-sourced relative to the others; a targeted follow-up search of specific firm engineering blogs (e.g., Jane Street, Two Sigma, Hudson River Trading) or r/quant / Quantitative Finance StackExchange threads was not completed within this research pass's tool budget and would materially strengthen this section.

## Adjacent markets entirely: sports betting +EV/arbitrage tools, weather derivatives, energy markets, crypto basis

### Takeaway
Of the four adjacent markets considered, retail sports-betting arbitrage/+EV tooling is the most clearly saturated and geofenced-irrelevant (the user cannot access Kalshi sports markets in Washington State, and the broader sportsbook arb/+EV tool market is already dominated by established players); weather derivatives are structurally closed to retail (90% OTC, standardized exchange access via CME is corporate/institutional); crypto basis trading is realistic and technically similar to the user's existing skill set but yields have compressed roughly 3–6x from their 2020–2021 peak and the space is far more competitive/institutional than it was.

### Cited Findings
- Arbitrage betting is explicitly characterized (by an industry source) as offering "smaller margins, more work, and sportsbooks that actively hunt arbitrage bettors, with returns working out to about 2-3% per bet," and the retail tool market is already led by established, well-resourced products: OddsJam ("the most popular arbitrage tool in the US market," scanning 100+ sportsbooks in real time), BetBurger ("the gold standard... outside the US"), and RebelBetting (offers automated bet placement) — [XCLSV: Best Arbitrage Betting Software 2026](https://xclsvmedia.com/best-arbitrage-betting-software-2026-complete-guide-to-arb-betting-tools/) (accessed 2026-09-18)
- "How many real edges appear per day depends on the day's card, the sports in season, and how many books you can bet in your state... no tool can manufacture an edge that the market is not offering" — [OddsShopper: Best Sports Betting Software 2026](https://www.oddsshopper.com/articles/comparisons/best-sports-betting-software) (accessed 2026-09-18)
- Weather derivatives: roughly 90% of the weather derivatives market is OTC; CME Group is "the undisputed hub for organized weather derivative trading" for standardized exchange contracts, but access is structured around "corporate end-users including energy retailers, airlines, construction firms, and agri-businesses" who access via exchange accounts or brokers, with retail access noted as likely limited to "synthetic exposure through intermediaries that handle know-your-customer and reporting" — [enmacc: Trading weather derivatives](https://enmacc.com/environmental/weather-derivatives/) and general search synthesis (accessed 2026-09-18). CME recorded "more than 260% growth in listed weather derivative contracts in 2023," with outstanding contracts ~48% higher in May 2024 than May 2023, and OTC "quanto" weather products reaching ~$25 billion notional in 2024 — showing the category is growing but the growth is institutional, not retail.
- Crypto basis trade (long spot BTC/ETH, short equivalent futures/perps): "earned 30-50% annualized yields with minimal risk in 2020-2021," but "by 2026 returns have compressed to 5-15% annualized in normal conditions, occasionally spiking higher" — [BackQuant: The Basis Trade Explained](https://www.backquant.com/learn/basis-trade) (accessed 2026-09-18)
- "Structural edges in crypto markets come from market fragmentation across exchanges, with funding rate dislocations, basis spreads between spot and futures, and cross-exchange latency creating exploitable inefficiencies that do not exist in mature equity markets," though the same source notes crypto markets have "matured significantly between 2020 and 2026, with deeper liquidity, tighter spreads, more institutional participation, and considerably more competition" — [Quantt: Crypto Quant Strategies 2026](https://www.quantt.co.uk/resources/crypto-quant-strategies-2026) (accessed 2026-09-18)
- Institutional ownership of the largest spot Bitcoin ETF (28%) is lower than in the S&P 500 ETF (58%), which the source frames as meaning "the retail footprint may make market flows more sensitive to sentiment" in crypto relative to traditional equities — same source

### Inferences
- Sports +EV/arbitrage tooling should be ranked as a poor fit for this specific user for two independent reasons: it is a saturated, professionally-tooled market generally (per the cited sources), and it is directly foreclosed by the stated Washington State geofencing constraint on Kalshi sports markets — even setting aside saturation, the user cannot practically access the sportsbook side either without using out-of-state/offshore books, which the constraints imply is out of scope (paper-trading-only, Kalshi-centric project).
- Weather derivatives (the CME/OTC institutional product, distinct from Kalshi's retail weather event contracts the user already trades) are not a realistic solo-dev entry point: the structural barrier is access (KYC'd broker relationships, institutional counterparties), not skill or tooling, so the existing codebase's strengths (backtesting, data pipelines) don't overcome the access barrier.
- Crypto basis trading is the most realistic of the four "adjacent markets entirely" for a solo dev with this skill set: it requires the same kind of skills already demonstrated (data pipelines, careful backtesting, fee/execution modelling) and retail access is straightforward (any exchange account), but the honest expectation-setting from the cited source is that yields have compressed 3-6x from the 2020-2021 era and the space now has "considerably more competition" — so it should be pursued, if at all, with the expectation of moderate (5-15% annualized) mechanical yield rather than a rediscovered inefficiency, and is a meaningfully different domain (crypto market microstructure, funding rates, exchange counterparty risk) from the prediction-market domain the existing codebase was built around, implying real new development cost rather than a pure re-use of existing infrastructure.

### Gaps
- No source specifically addressed energy markets (e.g., retail-accessible power/gas futures or weather-linked energy derivatives) as a solo-dev opportunity distinct from the general weather-derivatives finding; this sub-question is effectively unanswered.
- No source quantified the capital or infrastructure (e.g., minimum viable account size, specific exchange APIs) needed to execute a crypto basis trade profitably net of funding-rate volatility and exchange fees at solo-dev scale — the 5-15% annualized figure is a market-level average, not a return net of a small trader's real costs and slippage.
