# Practitioner Strategies for Making Money on Kalshi and Similar Prediction-Market Venues

## Kalshi's own market-maker / liquidity provider programs: terms, qualification, API/fee tiers

### Takeaway
Kalshi runs at least two distinct, non-overlapping incentive schemes — a discretionary, application-based **Market Maker Program** (reduced fees + adjusted position limits, in exchange for near-continuous quoting obligations) and an open-to-everyone **Liquidity Incentive Program** (a daily cash reward pool funded independently of trading fees, paid for resting two-sided orders). Neither publishes a fixed rebate rate; both are more accessible to a solo developer than "become a designated market maker at a big firm" implies, but the reward economics are opaque and actively being renegotiated by Kalshi as of 2026.

### Cited Findings
- Kalshi's Market Maker Program grants status "following a thorough review of financial resources, trading experience, and business reputation," and requires "98% of each 1h increment" of quoting availability across covered products (60+ markets spanning indices, crypto, sports, and economic indicators like CPI/jobs). Benefits are "reduced fees" and "certain adjusted position limits," conditional on maintaining liquidity; market makers "assume trading risk and may face losses like any other participant." — [How to Become a Market Maker on Kalshi | Kalshi Help Center](https://help.kalshi.com/en/articles/13823819-how-to-become-a-market-maker-on-kalshi)
- Separately, Kalshi's **Liquidity Incentive Program** pays $1–$1,000 per market per day (minimum payout $1.00) to any eligible U.S. member (not just designated MMs) who posts resting two-sided orders of 100–20,000 contracts that improve book depth; it runs through January 1, 2027. Eligibility excludes Kalshi affiliates/employees, IBs/FCMs and their customers, and non-U.S. users; a verified SSN is required for payouts above IRS reporting thresholds. Kalshi snapshots the order book once per second at a random instant and scores resting orders by size and closeness to a dynamic reference price; your share of the daily reward pool is your score divided by the total. — [Liquidity Incentive Program | Kalshi Help Center](https://help.kalshi.com/en/articles/13823851-liquidity-incentive-program)
- A March 2026 CFTC rule filing states the Market Maker Program's incentive rates "are being amended to account for the new transaction fee structure," that rebate rates/caps are "communicated in writing to Program Participants" (not published), adjustable "on a prospective basis with seven (7) days' notice," and that applicants "may be waitlisted indefinitely." — [Fee Waiver Program and Amendment to the Volume Incentive Program (CFTC filing)](https://www.cftc.gov/sites/default/files/filings/orgrules/24/06/rules0627242994.pdf) (search-summarized; filing not fetched directly, so exact 2026 text is unverified)
- A trader analysis (via X/Substack) claims that in the "open tier," a flat $0.005 per-contract reward against a $0.00204 taker fee on a 3-cent contract equals 245% of that fee — implying the reward can exceed the fee it's designed to offset for very cheap contracts. — [Navnoor Bawa, "Kalshi Publishes One Liquidity Subsidy and Seals the Other"](https://x.com/navnoorquant/article/2088369227283751256) (search-summarized only, not independently verified)
- Under the pre-April-2025 fee regime studied by academics, Kalshi charged Takers a fee of $0.07·P·(1−P) per contract (rounding up to the nearest cent) and charged Makers nothing; Kalshi began charging Makers fees too after April 2025. — [Bürgi, Deng & Whelan, "Makers and Takers: The Economics of the Kalshi Prediction Market," GWU/UCD Working Paper 2026-001](https://www2.gwu.edu/~forcpgm/2026-001.pdf), p.6
- An independent analysis of the liquidity program speculates "small scrappy teams" could earn "six figures a month" by specializing in under-covered market categories where "one shop can't cover everything well," since the program rewards quote-tightness and consistency rather than raw capital — but the author explicitly says they don't yet know how incentive pool sizes are communicated or whether professional market makers will simply dilute individual retail payouts. — [ufoholdings.substack.com, "Kalshi's New Liquidity Incentives"](https://ufoholdings.substack.com/p/kalshis-new-liquidity-incentives)

### Inferences
- The two programs target different populations: the Market Maker Program is closer to a designated-MM arrangement (needs a "financial resources/trading experience" review, waitlists) and is plausibly closed in practice to solo retail; the Liquidity Incentive Program is explicitly open to ordinary account holders and is the more realistic entry point for a solo developer wanting exchange-subsidized quoting income — but its payouts are a shared pool, so payouts shrink as more (including professional) participants join.
- Because rebate rates are private and change with 7 days' notice, any strategy built around "the rebate exceeds my adverse-selection cost" needs to be re-validated frequently rather than assumed stable.

### Gaps
- No official, current, published fee schedule numbers were retrieved (the Kalshi fee schedule PDF failed to download intact during this research — the file returned was not a valid PDF, likely due to a redirect or bot-block). The precise 2026 maker/taker fee rates, and the exact current Market Maker Program rebate schedule, remain unverified from primary sources here.
- No data found on how many individual (non-firm) accounts have been approved into the Market Maker Program, or what capital/volume threshold triggers approval versus waitlisting.

## Cross-venue arbitrage (Kalshi vs Polymarket vs sportsbooks vs CME/futures) in practice

### Takeaway
Cross-venue arbitrage between Kalshi and Polymarket is real and actively discussed/tooled in 2026, but nearly every credible source frames it as (a) rapidly compressing in margin as bots and institutional liquidity arrive, and (b) laden with execution risk — headline "arbs" frequently turn out to be false positives once contract terms, mutual-exclusivity, and fill risk are checked. No source in this research quantified capital requirements or latency needs with hard numbers; claims of specific dollar profits come from marketing-oriented blogs, not verified accounts.
- Sportsbook and CME/futures cross-arbitrage was not documented in citable, credible sources found in this pass (see Gaps).

### Cited Findings
- Marketing/tooling sites (ClawArbs, LaikaLabs, TradingVPS) claim: Polymarket-Kalshi arbitrage rates averaged ~9.6% across six scans in an "ArbBets" report, with a forecast that margins compress to 3–8% "as more bots enter the market and platforms adjust pricing engines," with profitability increasingly dependent on "speed, capital efficiency, and fee optimization." — [ArbBets/ClawArbs, "Kalshi vs Polymarket Arbitrage: Bot Guide 2026"](https://clawarbs.com/blog/kalshi-vs-polymarket-arbitrage/) (marketing source; unverified methodology, treat as a claim not a fact)

- A more careful independent analysis (not a tool vendor) describes cross-venue scanning methodology and stresses that meeting the "combined cost under 100¢" threshold is necessary but **not sufficient**: in one worked example (an artist-album-release market), a scanner flagged five apparent Kalshi/Polymarket arbitrage candidates overnight, but only three survived scrutiny as "genuinely risk-free," and the article notes "the 'best' of the five isn't an arbitrage at all" once contract wording and mutual exclusivity were checked properly. — [algocapital.substack.com, "What Happens When Polymarket and Kalshi Disagree"](https://algocapital.substack.com/p/what-happens-when-polymarket-and)

An "environmental hedge fund" reportedly completed the first institutional block trade on Kalshi with Jump Trading as counterparty. — [Finance Magnates / DeFi Rate on Jump Trading](https://www.financemagnates.com/forex/prop-firm-jump-trading-to-take-stakes-in-kalshi-and-polymarket-report/); [Laika AI on X re: Susquehanna](https://x.com/Laika_ai/status/2064662950141423972); [Tradermath, "Prediction Markets Trading at Quant Firms"](https://www.tradermath.org/articles/prediction-markets-trading-at-quant-firms)

### Inferences
- The presence of Jump Trading, Susquehanna, Jane Street, Wintermute, DRW and IMC as active market makers on one or both venues by 2026 strongly implies that persistent, easily-scanned cross-venue arbitrage (the kind advertised by retail bot vendors) is being competed down quickly wherever it appears in liquid markets; what's left is likely to be in thin, low-volume, or newly-listed markets where institutional attention hasn't yet arrived — consistent with the GW/UCD academic paper's finding (below) that Kalshi's pricing anomalies persist specifically because volumes are too small to attract professional capital.
- Genuine cross-venue arbitrage execution requires accounts (and funded capital) on both Kalshi and Polymarket simultaneously, live order-book feeds from both, and automated execution — this is a solo-developer-buildable stack technically, but the *margin* available after accounting for fees (Polymarket now charges fees as of Jan 2026), settlement-timing mismatches, and the false-positive rate documented above (2 of 5 flagged opportunities were not real arbitrage in one worked example) means expected per-trade edge is likely thin and highly sensitive to execution discipline.
- Washington State's geofencing of Kalshi sports/politics/culture markets is a material constraint here: much of the retail cross-venue arb literature focuses on sports and political events, which the reader cannot legally trade on Kalshi from WA. Economics/finance/weather markets (the reader's available categories) are exactly the categories the academic paper below finds to have some of the *smaller* mispricing coefficients (see Table 8 in the next section) and are less discussed in the arbitrage-bot marketing literature — suggesting less crowded but also potentially thinner opportunity.

## Within-venue structural trades: bracket ladders, YES/NO parity, correlated-event mispricing

### Takeaway
The strongest, most rigorously documented within-venue structural finding is not a "free money" bracket-sum arbitrage — it's a persistent, statistically robust **favorite-longshot bias** documented by peer-reviewed-style academic research using Kalshi's own transaction data through April 2025: cheap (longshot) contracts are overpriced and lose money on average; expensive (favorite) contracts are underpriced and post small positive average returns, especially for Makers. Kalshi's single-market YES/NO parity (they always sum to ~100¢ by construction) rules out the "buy YES and NO under a dollar" trade within one binary market; genuine structural edge, where it exists, is in mutually-exclusive multi-outcome brackets not summing correctly, and even that requires careful mutual-exclusivity gating to avoid false signals.

### Cited Findings
- Using 46,282 Kalshi Yes contracts (313,972 total Yes/No prices) from Kalshi's inception in 2021 through April 2025, an academic working paper (Bürgi, Deng & Whelan, George Washington University/UCD, Feb 2026) finds Kalshi prices display "a clear favorite–longshot bias: low-price contracts win far less often than required to break even after fees, while high-price contracts win more often and yield small positive returns." Investors who buy contracts costing under 10¢ lose over 60% of their money on average; contracts above 50¢ show statistically significant small positive returns; the overall average pre-fee return across all Kalshi contracts is about **−20%**. — [Bürgi, Deng & Whelan, "Makers and Takers: The Economics of the Kalshi Prediction Market," GWU Working Paper 2026-001, Jan/Feb 2026](https://www2.gwu.edu/~forcpgm/2026-001.pdf)
- The same paper is the first to exploit Kalshi's unique data field identifying which side of every trade was Maker vs. Taker (avoiding the noisy Lee-Ready trade-classification algorithm used elsewhere in market microstructure research). It finds Makers earn meaningfully better returns than Takers: average post-fee return for Makers was **−9.64%**, versus **−31.46%** for Takers, a difference that is "extremely" statistically significant. Makers who buy contracts priced ≥50¢ average a **+2.6%** post-fee return. — same source, Section 4 and Figure 6
- The favorite-longshot bias holds across every sub-sample tested: by category (financials, crypto, climate/weather, politics, entertainment, economics — all reject the null of unbiased pricing, p<0.01 in most), by trading-volume quintile (bias is *not* smaller in higher-volume markets — "no evidence of prices in higher-volume markets being more efficient predictors"), by transaction-size quintile, and by year 2021–2025 (bias persists every year, though the coefficient is smaller/less significant in 2025 data, suggesting *some* narrowing over time). — same source, Tables 4–9
- The paper's structural model shows the bias requires genuine probability-misperception (over-weighting of small probabilities, calibrated at β≈0.09), not just heterogeneous-but-unbiased beliefs — a model with unbiased beliefs (β=0) predicts the *opposite* pattern (Makers profiting most on cheap contracts), which is not what the data show.
- The paper directly asks "why hasn't this been arbitraged away?" and gives three candidate reasons: (1) **small volumes** — even the top decile of Kalshi markets averaged only $526,245 in total final volume, and order-book depth at any instant is far smaller (their worked CPI example shows only a few thousand dollars available at the best few price levels), so professional capital can't deploy size without moving the market itself; (2) **riskiness** — the standard deviation of Maker returns on ≥50¢ contracts is 33%, dwarfing the 2.6% average return, and standard "proper risk aversion" utility theory (Pratt & Zeckhauser 1987, building on Samuelson's 1963 fallacy-of-large-numbers argument) implies rational risk-averse traders may correctly reject repeated small-edge, high-variance bets even when the Law of Large Numbers would suggest safety; (3) **lack of information** — participants may simply not know about the bias yet, and the authors explicitly flag it as an open question whether this anomaly will shrink now that it's been published (citing precedent of other market anomalies disappearing post-publication). — same source, Section 6
- On multi-outcome bracket structure specifically: independent (non-academic) trading-blog analysis states Kalshi exposes a mutual-exclusivity flag on events, and that scanning bracket ladders for "doesn't sum to 100" mispricing is only valid when gated on that flag — non-mutually-exclusive multi-outcome markets (e.g., "which phrases will the Fed chair say," where many can independently resolve YES) legitimately sum to more than 100¢ and are not arbitrage. — [trevorlasn.com/botforkalshi.com, "How Prediction Market Arbitrage Works"](https://www.trevorlasn.com/blog/how-prediction-market-polymarket-kalshi-arbitrage-works)

### Inferences
- The academic evidence directly supports the reader's own finding that "taking liquidity on weather brackets loses (spread > mispricing)": the GW/UCD paper's category breakdown shows Climate & Weather has one of the *smaller* favorite-longshot coefficients (ψ=0.031, similar to Financials at 0.032, both smaller than Crypto's 0.058 or "Other" at 0.053) — i.e., weather is one of the more efficiently-priced categories, leaving less room for a taker to overcome the spread.
- The paper's "why hasn't this been arbitraged away" analysis (thin order books, real risk, possible retail unawareness) reads as a direct explanation of why the reader's passive-selling / liquidity-taking experiments underperformed: acting as a Maker on higher-priced (≥50¢) contracts is the only leg of this documented anomaly with a positive expected return, and even that is a thin (+2.6%), high-variance (33% SD) edge that requires being filled at scale to matter — which the reader's likely small individual order sizes make hard to exploit meaningfully, and which is exactly the same regime (posting resting orders, avoiding paying the spread) that Kalshi's Liquidity Incentive Program pays a separate cash rebate for.
- Because the paper explicitly logs the *possibility* that publication itself erodes the bias, and because Kalshi's fee structure changed in April 2025 (Makers now also pay fees, unlike in the paper's sample period), the exact magnitudes above (β=0.09, −20% average return, +2.6% Maker edge on ≥50¢ contracts) should be treated as descriptive of 2021–April-2025 behavior and not assumed to hold unchanged through 2026 without re-testing on current data.

### Gaps
- No dataset or study covering **post-April-2025 (current fee regime) or 2026** Kalshi pricing was found to confirm whether the favorite-longshot bias and Maker/Taker return gap persist under the new maker-fee structure — this is a significant gap given the reader's own 2026 paper-trading context.
- No quantitative study of live bracket-ladder ("sum ≠ 100¢") frequency, size, or persistence (how many cents of edge, how often, how fast filled) was found — the mutual-exclusivity caveat is well-documented qualitatively but no source gave hard numbers on how often genuine (correctly-gated) bracket mispricing appears or how quickly it's arbitraged.
- No study of correlated-event mispricing specifically (e.g., a Fed-decision market priced inconsistently with a related CPI market) was found in this pass.

## News/data-latency strategies: realistic retail latency edge

### Takeaway
The retail latency edge on Kalshi is bounded by ordinary internet physics (roughly 10+ ms round-trip even to a nearby data center) and by Kalshi's own trading-halt/circuit-breaker mechanics around scheduled releases; no credible source found here claims retail participants can win a sub-second race against institutional infrastructure on CPI/jobs/FOMC releases. The more durable "retail edge" described in credible sources is not speed — it's exploiting the same favorite-longshot / base-rate mispricing documented above, which persists regardless of how fast you can click.

### Cited Findings
- Kalshi itself, and Fed-affiliated academic research, treat Kalshi's CPI/jobs/FOMC/GDP contracts as legitimate high-frequency macro-forecasting tools: a Federal Reserve Board working paper ("Kalshi and the Rise of Macro Markets," Diercks, Katz & Wright, 2026) finds Kalshi's real-time distributional forecasts for CPI, Fed rate decisions, GDP, and unemployment "matched or outperformed" traditional benchmarks, with "a perfect track record on the most likely rate outcome pre-FOMC meetings" in their sample. — [Federal Reserve, "Kalshi and the Rise of Macro Markets"](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/kalshi-and-the-rise-of-macro-markets.htm); also on [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34702) and [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6333085)
- On latency physics: one millisecond of network latency corresponds to roughly 124 miles of fiber; a Chicago-to-Columbus link (~300 miles) implies roughly 10 ms round-trip at the physical minimum — an explicit warning against believing marketing claims of "sub-millisecond" edges to Kalshi from any colocation. — [search-summarized, Kalshi trading-VPS content](https://tradoxvps.com/best-vps-for-kalshi-trading-bots/)
- The GW/UCD academic paper (above) separately finds forecast accuracy (mean absolute error) *improves* smoothly as a Kalshi market approaches its close, with "a steep drop" on the closing day itself — meaning the information-quality gap that any trader (fast or slow) is exploiting shrinks mechanically as the release approaches, independent of anyone's execution speed. — [Bürgi, Deng & Whelan, Figure 4](https://www2.gwu.edu/~forcpgm/2026-001.pdf)
- The same paper explicitly frames the primary, robust retail-exploitable feature of Kalshi pricing as the favorite-longshot bias (a base-rate mispricing that a patient trader posting as Maker on ≥50¢ contracts can partially capture), not a speed-based edge. — same source

### Inferences
- Given Kalshi's markets are typically thin (median contract volume ~$9,000 per the GW/UCD data) and physical latency to Kalshi's infrastructure is on the order of several to tens of milliseconds even for a professional colocated server, a solo developer on a home/consumer internet connection (likely 20–100+ ms round-trip plus OS/application jitter) has essentially no realistic latency edge against professional market makers (Jump Trading, Susquehanna, Jane Street, etc.) now active in the space for scheduled-release trading. The "edge" available to a retail participant with normal internet is informational/statistical (base-rate mispricing, favorite-longshot bias, category-specific pricing errors) rather than temporal.
- For the reader specifically: since sports in-play and political markets are geofenced off in Washington, and weather-station-update latency games are the exact strategy already shown to lose (spread > mispricing), the CPI/jobs/economics category is the most promising within the "data-latency" family — but the credible evidence available says the edge there is Kalshi's own pricing being *unbiased and accurate* at the aggregate/forecasting level (good for the Fed, not obviously exploitable for profit) rather than a documented retail trading edge.

### Gaps
- No source directly measured or reported an actual dollar P&L for a retail trader specifically running a "trade the CPI print before the crowd repriced it" strategy on Kalshi. This is inferred to be difficult/marginal from the physics and market-thinness evidence above, but no direct disconfirming or confirming trader account was found.
- No information was found on Kalshi's specific trading-halt or circuit-breaker rules around scheduled data releases (e.g., whether the market pauses at release time, for how long, and how re-opening prices are set) — this matters a great deal for any latency-style strategy and should be checked directly against Kalshi's own market rules/help center before attempting it.

## What credible practitioners say does NOT work for retail — and why

### Takeaway
The most credible, hardest evidence against a specific retail strategy is the academic paper's core finding itself: buying cheap ("longshot") contracts as a Taker is a documented, statistically robust way to lose money (average −31.46% for Takers, worse than −60% on sub-10¢ contracts), and this persists across every category, every year 2021–2025, and every volume/trade-size bucket tested — it is not a fluke of one period or market type. Separately, sports-market-making research and quant-firm reporting both stress that retail participants get systematically limited/outcompeted once they show an edge, and that true cross-book/latency arbitrage needs infrastructure (API access across venues, sub-second execution, meaningful capital) that individuals structurally lack.

### Cited Findings
- The GW/UCD paper's core, most policy-relevant conclusion: "Kalshi's prices exhibit a strong favorite–longshot bias... Returns are substantially negative for cheap contracts and modestly positive for more expensive ones... users should be aware that market prices — particularly for low-probability events — reflect both information and behavioral distortions" and should not be treated as unbiased probability estimates for trading purposes. — [Bürgi, Deng & Whelan, Section 7 Conclusion](https://www2.gwu.edu/~forcpgm/2026-001.pdf)
- A sports-betting-exchange market-microstructure analysis states plainly that "bookmakers and exchanges systematically identify and limit winning traders," which is why institutional players (e.g., Susquehanna's Nellie Analytics unit) seek dedicated partnerships/equity stakes rather than trading as ordinary retail accounts — implying a retail account that becomes consistently profitable risks being limited or restricted, a standard sports-book practice that may or may not extend to Kalshi's structure (Kalshi is an exchange, not a bookmaker, which is structurally different, but the account-restriction dynamic is worth flagging as a risk). — [navnoorbawaresearch.com, "Market Making in Sports Betting"](https://www.navnoorbawaresearch.com/p/market-making-in-sports-betting-how)
- The same analysis states that liquidity fragmentation itself caps strategy scale even for professionals: individual UK horse races average only ~£500k matched volume and major football matches rarely exceed single-digit millions, which is "precisely why whale-sized hedge funds fail while proprietary shops succeed" in this space — a structural argument that applies with even more force to Kalshi's much smaller markets (median $8,982 staked per contract per the GW/UCD data). — same source
- A prediction-market SEO/review site (lower credibility, but directionally consistent with the academic findings) states "there are 2.9 unprofitable users for each profitable one" on Kalshi, i.e., a claimed ~26% of users are net profitable — this specific ratio is unsourced/unverifiable and should be treated as an unverified claim, not fact, but is directionally consistent with a market that has a systematic bias favoring a subset of participants (Makers on expensive contracts) over another (Takers on cheap contracts). — [botforkalshi.com, "How to Make Money on Kalshi"](https://www.botforkalshi.com/blog/how-to-make-money-on-kalshi) (low-credibility source; flagged as unverified)

### Inferences
- Combining the academic paper's category breakdown with the reader's own tested strategies: the reader has independently rediscovered, empirically, exactly the finding the academic paper documents theoretically — that taking liquidity (buying at the ask) is adversely priced against the taker, especially on cheap/longshot contracts, and that the spread on efficiently-priced categories (weather, financials) already contains most of the available edge, leaving nothing for a liquidity-taker to capture.
- "What doesn't work for retail" as documented here is specifically: (1) buying cheap longshot contracts hoping for a lottery-ticket payoff (documented −60%+ average loss), (2) expecting to win a latency race against firms now running dedicated prediction-market desks, (3) expecting cross-venue "free money" arbitrage signals to be real without manually verifying mutual exclusivity and contract-term equivalence, and (4) sizing up "safe" high-probability Maker positions to exploit the +2.6% edge without accounting for its 33% standard deviation — i.e., treating a positive-expected-value edge as if it were a Kelly-safe or low-variance one.

### Gaps
- No first-person, named practitioner interview or podcast transcript (e.g., an Odd Lots episode featuring a Kalshi/Polymarket trader) was retrieved in this research pass discussing what they explicitly tried and abandoned; this would strengthen the "doesn't work" case with direct practitioner testimony rather than academic inference. Time/tool-call budget did not allow pursuing this further.

## Tax/regulatory/operational overheads

### Takeaway
Section 1256's favorable 60/40 capital-gains treatment for Kalshi contracts is an unresolved, aggressive tax position as of 2026 — the IRS has issued no direct guidance, and there is a live legal tension between Kalshi's litigation position (that its contracts are *not* swaps, which are excluded from 1256) and its potential tax position (that its contracts *are* 1256 contracts). Fees are asymmetric and were the subject of an academic study cited above (post-2025, both Makers and Takers now pay). Withdrawal frictions are modest (1-3 business days ACH) but capital committed to open positions is locked until settlement, and settlement disputes over ambiguous real-world events are a documented, recurring source of user complaints, including at least one active class-action.

### Cited Findings
- Section 1256 treatment gives 60% long-term / 40% short-term capital gains treatment regardless of actual holding period, plus mark-to-market at year end; Form 6781 is the relevant IRS form if a taxpayer takes this position. — [ContractTax, "Form 6781 and Kalshi"](https://www.contracttax.com/guides/kalshi-form-6781); [ContractTax, "The Section 1256 60/40 Rule Explained"](https://www.contracttax.com/guides/section-1256-60-40-explained)
- "Nobody can promise you the 60/40 rate yet" — Kalshi and Polymarket contracts trading on a CFTC-designated contract market satisfies only half of the Section 1256 statutory test; the IRS has issued no revenue ruling, notice, private letter ruling, or other formal guidance on whether event contracts qualify. Critically, Section 1256(b)(2)(B) (added by Dodd-Frank) excludes swaps from 1256 treatment, and the ongoing litigation Kalshi has been winning (regarding its right to offer sports/political contracts against state objections) turns on characterizing its contracts as *not* swaps — creating a direct tension where the litigation win could undercut the tax position. Treating Kalshi contracts as 1256 is explicitly described as "an aggressive tax position." — [Coselite, "Section 1256 and Event Contracts (2026): Does 60/40 Apply?"](https://coselite.com/blog/section-1256-event-contracts)
- Withdrawal mechanics: ACH withdrawals are free and settle in 1-3 business days (up to 5 in some cases depending on the receiving bank); most markets credit the Kalshi account balance within hours to 48 hours of the resolution source publishing. Funds are only illiquid while actively committed to open (unsettled) positions — settled funds and uncommitted cash can be withdrawn immediately. — [search-summarized from multiple Kalshi guide sites, including tradetheoutcome.com and pillarlabai.com]
- Common complaints center on withdrawal delays tied to standard KYC/AML holds (described as "annoying, not fraudulent") and "settlement rage" — disputes where users believe an event has clearly resolved but Kalshi has not yet paid out, often due to ambiguous real-world resolution criteria. A specific class-action complaint was reportedly filed over Kalshi's settlement methodology for an "Ali Khamenei out as Supreme Leader?" market. — [search-summarized, oddsshopper.com and related coverage] (the class-action claim itself is not independently verified beyond the search snippet — treat as reported-but-unconfirmed)
- Fee structure changed materially in 2025: pre-April-2025, only Takers paid Kalshi's $0.07·P·(1−P) fee; Makers paid nothing. Kalshi began charging Makers fees as well after April 2025, which is exactly why the academic study's sample cuts off there. — [Bürgi, Deng & Whelan, Section 2.2](https://www2.gwu.edu/~forcpgm/2026-001.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a solo trader (the reader), the practical tax overhead question is not "does 1256 apply" in the abstract but "am I willing to take an aggressive, unresolved position that could be challenged on audit, particularly given Kalshi's own litigation posture cuts against the swaps-exclusion argument working in the trader's favor." A conservative approach (ordinary short-term capital gains / no 1256) is the lower-risk default until IRS guidance issues.
- The April 2025 fee change (Makers now pay fees) means any of the paper's Maker-side profitability figures (e.g., +2.6% on ≥50¢ contracts) should be treated as an *upper bound* on what's achievable in 2026 under the current fee regime, since that historical edge was partly a product of Makers paying zero fees.

### Gaps
- No current (2026) precise fee schedule numbers (post-Maker-fee-change) were retrieved from a primary Kalshi source in this pass — the official fee schedule PDF fetch failed. This should be independently re-verified directly from Kalshi's help center or fee schedule PDF before modeling strategy economics.
- No information was found on whether/how 1099 reporting practically interacts with the 1256 question in 2026 (i.e., does Kalshi issue 1099-B with 1256 characterization, or does the trader have to self-elect), which materially affects how "aggressive" the position actually is in practice.
